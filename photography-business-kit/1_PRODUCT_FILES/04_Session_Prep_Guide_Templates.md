## What to Wear

This is the section I get asked about most — and I get it. Clothing decisions feel high-stakes when you know photos are going to live on your walls for years. Let me make this as simple as possible for you.

### The Golden Rule: Coordinate, Don't Match

Perfectly matching outfits — everyone in the same color, same shade, same style — used to be the standard. It isn't anymore, and for good reason: coordinated-but-not-matching families look like a real family. They look natural. They look like *you.*

The goal is a color palette, not a uniform.

**How to build your palette:** Pick 2–3 anchor colors that feel like your family's personality. Then let each person dress within that palette in a way that suits them. Your teenager gets to wear something they actually like. Your toddler can wear something comfortable enough that they won't melt down over it. Everyone wins.

### Color Palette Ideas

Here are five palette suggestions you can use as a starting point. These aren't rules — they're inspiration.

**Palette 1: Warm and Natural**
Cream, caramel, rust, and olive green. Think linen textures, warm tones, a cozy autumn afternoon. This palette photographs beautifully in golden hour light and in wooded or park settings.

**Palette 2: Cool and Classic**
Navy, white, soft grey, and a touch of blush. Timeless, clean, and elegant. Works especially well for beach sessions or light-filled studios.

**Palette 3: Earthy and Rich**
Terracotta, sage, dusty mauve, and warm brown. This combination feels organic and layered — great for families who want something a little more editorial.

**Palette 4: Soft and Romantic**
Ivory, blush, dusty blue, and lavender. Beautiful for spring sessions, wildflower fields, or any soft, dreamy location.

**Palette 5: Bold and Grounded**
Deep burgundy, forest green, camel, and charcoal. Rich, sophisticated, and striking — especially beautiful in fall.

Once you have a palette in mind, lay everything out on the bed together before the session. Look at the group as a whole. If one person's outfit is fighting for attention, swap it out.

### Textures and Patterns That Photograph Well

**Yes to:** Linen, chambray, soft knits, subtle florals, small-scale plaid, eyelet, embroidery, and light layers. These all add visual interest without distracting from faces.

**Use sparingly:** Large-scale plaid, wide stripes, and bold prints. These can work, but make sure only one person in the family is wearing them — they don't need to compete.

**No to:** Neon colors, fluorescent anything, or any color that's so bright it draws the eye away from your faces.

### What to Avoid

- **Logos and graphics.** Text, brand logos, sports team names — they date a photo instantly and pull the viewer's eye to the shirt instead of the person wearing it. This includes your favorite band tee. Save it for another day.
- **All-white or all-black for the whole family.** White can work beautifully on one or two people, but an entirely white family in full sun can blow out and lose detail. All-black can go flat in photos. Mix it in — don't lead with it.
- **Neon and very bright colors.** These cast color onto skin, making editing more difficult and skin tones look off.
- **Busy patterns on multiple people.** One person in a floral or plaid? Great. Three people? Chaotic. Keep patterns to one or two people in the group.

### Clothing Fit Tips

Clothes that fit well photograph well. It's that simple — and it cuts both ways. Anything too tight will show every pull and seam in unflattering ways. Anything too baggy can make people look shapeless in photos.

- **Avoid anything you'll need to adjust constantly.** That strapless top that keeps slipping, the waistband that needs pulling up, the collar that never sits right — skip it. You want to be present in the session, not managing your clothes.
- **Iron or steam everything in advance.** Wrinkles that look minor in person show up very clearly in photos.
- **For young children:** Comfort is king. A toddler who is physically uncomfortable will be emotionally uncomfortable. Choose soft fabrics, avoid scratchy tags or tight waistbands, and let them wear something they're used to moving in.

### Footwear

Yes, footwear matters — even when I'm shooting from the waist up most of the time. We will get full-body and walking shots where shoes are visible.

- **Clean shoes.** Scrub the soles, wipe off the scuffs. This sounds obvious; people forget.
- **Coordinate with the palette.** White sneakers work with almost everything. Nude or tan sandals are versatile. Bold-colored shoes draw attention downward.
- **Avoid flip-flops** unless we're shooting at the beach and it genuinely makes sense for the session.
- **For kids:** Make sure they can actually run and play in their shoes. A toddler in dress shoes who can't move freely is a recipe for a meltdown.

### Accessories

**Do:** Simple jewelry, delicate necklaces, stud earrings, hats that feel natural to your family's style, a meaningful bracelet. Accessories add personality.

**Don't:** Oversized statement pieces that compete with faces, excessive jewelry on children (it can be a distraction and a choking hazard), or matching accessories that feel costume-y.

**A special note:** If you have a piece of jewelry with sentimental value — a grandmother's ring, a necklace from your wedding day — wear it. These details matter, and I love photographing them.

## Tips for Kids

This is the section where I want you to exhale.

### Toddlers and Reluctant Participants

Here is what I know after photographing hundreds of families: **the best photos almost never happen when everyone is perfectly still and looking at the camera.** They happen in the in-between moments. A toddler running toward you with their arms out. Siblings whispering something to each other. A kid who suddenly decides to show me their best spin move.

I am very good at working with kids who don't want to cooperate — because almost all of them don't want to cooperate at first, and that's completely normal. Give me 10 minutes with a reluctant toddler and I'll usually have them chasing me around and laughing.

**What actually helps:**

- **Schedule the session around nap time.** A well-rested toddler is a different creature entirely from an overtired one. If your child naps at 1 p.m., don't schedule us for noon. Schedule us for 3 p.m., after the nap.
- **Feed them first.** Hungry children are unhappy children. A small snack 30 minutes before the session is ideal.
- **Don't build it up too much.** The more you tell a child "you have to be good for pictures," the more pressure they feel. Keep it casual. "We're going to go to the park and hang out — and our friend is going to take some photos." That's all they need to know.

### On Bribing

You can absolutely bribe your children. I support this fully.

Just don't bribe them with sugar or candy right before we shoot. Sugar spikes lead to crashes, and stained clothing leads to frustration.

**What works better:** Promise a favorite activity after the session — ice cream on the way home, a movie night, extra screen time, dinner at their favorite restaurant. Future rewards keep them invested without disrupting the session itself.

### Let Kids Be Kids

The goal is not a perfectly posed, perfectly still photograph of your family. The goal is photographs that look like your family. If your 4-year-old wants to pick up a stick and examine it for five minutes, let them. Some of the best family photos I've ever taken involved a child completely doing their own thing while the parents looked at each other and smiled.

Trust me. Trust the process. Give yourself permission to let go of "perfect."

### Managing Expectations About Poses

Children under 5 do not reliably "pose." They do not hold still on command. They may look in completely the wrong direction, or burst into tears right when the light is perfect, or suddenly become obsessed with a rock. This is completely normal and completely fine. I will capture the real moments — the laughter, the chaos, the love — and those will be your favorite images.

## On the Day Itself

### Morning and Afternoon Logistics

- **Protect nap time.** If the session is in the late afternoon or evening, make sure young children get their usual naps that day. Skip the nap, and you'll feel it by the time we start.
- **Eat a normal meal.** Don't skip lunch because you're nervous. Hungry parents get short-tempered, and kids absolutely pick up on that energy.
- **Get everyone dressed before you leave the house.** Don't plan to change in a parking lot unless absolutely necessary. Traveling in the session clothes keeps everyone looking put-together.

### A Note on Perfection

I want to say this clearly: **I am not here to take perfect photos. I am here to take true ones.**

On the day of your session, something will probably not go exactly as planned. A child will have a tantrum. Hair will get messy. Someone's outfit won't look quite right once you're all standing together. And none of that matters.

What I'm looking for is the love that's already there — the way you look at your kids, the way your kids pile on top of each other, the way your family actually exists in the world. That's what I photograph. And that's what you'll treasure.

### What I'll Do to Make Everyone Comfortable

When we start the session, I'll take a few minutes just to talk and let everyone get used to me and the camera before I start shooting in earnest. I'll give you gentle direction, suggest prompts and activities that get natural reactions, and keep things moving so kids don't lose interest. If someone needs a snack break, we'll take one. If we need to stop and chase a toddler around for two minutes, we'll do that. We're flexible. We're having fun.

## Getting Ready: Detail Shots and Portraits

### Set These Aside the Night Before

The first hour of wedding morning coverage is dedicated to detail shots — and these are some of the most important photographs of the day. Before I arrive, please gather the following items and set them in one place (a clean surface, a windowsill with good light, or a pretty tray works perfectly):

- **Rings** — both engagement ring and wedding bands, cleaned and polished
- **Invitation suite** — the full set, including envelope, details card, and any ribbon, wax seal, or vellum wrap
- **Shoes** — both pairs if possible
- **Bouquet** — or a single bloom if the florist hasn't delivered yet
- **Jewelry** — earrings, necklaces, hair accessories
- **Perfume bottle** — a beautiful perfume bottle is a wonderful detail shot
- **Veil**
- **Something meaningful** — a handwritten note from your partner, something borrowed, a heirloom piece

These shots take about 20–30 minutes and should happen at the very start of coverage, before everyone is dressed and the room gets busy.

### The Getting-Ready Space

The getting-ready environment makes a significant difference in your photos. Here's what works:

**The ideal space has:**
- Natural light from a large window (face the window, not away from it)
- Minimal clutter and personal items in the background
- Enough room for the hair and makeup artist to work and for me to move around

**Before I arrive:**
- Move any suitcases, garbage bags, shopping bags, and hotel furniture out of the frame (push it to a corner or another room)
- Hang the wedding dress somewhere prominent with good light — near a window or on a clean door frame
- Ask bridesmaids and family members to get dressed before I start covering the final moments, or be ready to change quickly

**Who should be in the room:** Immediate wedding party, parents if they'll be part of the getting-ready coverage, and the hair/makeup artist. A crowded, chaotic room is harder to photograph beautifully. Aunts, cousins, and friends are wonderful — but they don't all need to be in the bridal suite.

### Getting-Ready Timeline: How Much Time You Actually Need

This is where most couples underestimate. Here is a realistic breakdown:

| Task | Time |
|------|------|
| Detail shots | 20–30 minutes |
| Photographer arrives / sets up | 10 minutes |
| Hair (per person) | 45–60 minutes |
| Makeup (per person) | 45–60 minutes |
| Getting into dress (with helpers) | 20–30 minutes |
| Pre-ceremony portraits with wedding party | 20–30 minutes |
| Buffer for the unexpected | 20–30 minutes |

If you have 4 bridesmaids all getting hair and makeup done, plan accordingly. The number one cause of a rushed wedding day timeline is underestimating getting-ready time. **When in doubt, start earlier.**

## The First Look: Pros and Cons

### What Is a First Look?

A "first look" is a private, planned moment before the ceremony where you and your partner see each other for the first time on your wedding day. Your photographer is present to capture the reaction — the tears, the laughter, the long embrace — but no one else is around.

### Why Many Photographers Recommend It

- **Emotional payoff.** The first look is often more genuinely emotional than the aisle moment, because there's no audience, no performance pressure — just two people seeing each other.
- **It frees your timeline.** If you do a first look, you can complete all your couple portraits and many family formal photos before the ceremony. This means cocktail hour is actually cocktail hour for you — you attend your own party instead of being whisked away for photos.
- **Your photographer gets more time with you.** More time means more variety, more locations, more comfort in front of the camera.

### How to Decide

A first look is not right for everyone. Some couples feel strongly about the tradition of seeing each other for the first time at the altar, and that is completely valid and beautiful. If you choose not to do a first look, your couple portraits will happen between the ceremony and reception — which means a shorter portrait window and potentially missing part of cocktail hour.

Talk to me about what feels right for you. I'll help you build a timeline around your choice.

---

## Portrait Session Timing: Golden Hour

### What Is Golden Hour?

Golden hour is the 45–60 minutes just before sunset when the sun is low in the sky and the light is soft, warm, and flattering. It is, without exaggeration, the most beautiful light of the day — and it's why photographers plan around it obsessively.

### How to Build It Into Your Timeline

If you want golden hour couple portraits (and I highly recommend it), we need to carve out 20–30 minutes during this window, which means slipping away from your reception briefly. Most couples who do this are gone for 20 minutes and come back with the most breathtaking photos of the day.

**To make this work:**
- Know your sunset time ([I will provide this based on your date and location])
- Schedule dinner to be winding down around 30–45 minutes before sunset
- Let your wedding coordinator know this is planned so they can help facilitate the exit

### Couple Portrait Time Recommendations

- **Before ceremony (if first look):** 30–45 minutes for couple portraits
- **Between ceremony and reception:** 20–30 minutes (if no first look, this window should be longer — 45–60 minutes)
- **Golden hour departure during reception:** 20–30 minutes
- **Family formals:** 30–45 minutes (see below)

---

## Family Formal Photos

Family formals are the group shots — parents, grandparents, siblings, extended family. They are important, they can be exhausting to coordinate, and the difference between smooth and chaotic is almost entirely in the preparation.

### Create a Shot List

Before your wedding day, send me a shot list of every family grouping you want. Be specific. Instead of "immediate family," write:

- Bride + Groom + Bride's parents
- Bride + Groom + Groom's parents
- Bride + Groom + both sets of parents
- Bride + Groom + Bride's siblings
- Bride + Groom + Groom's siblings + spouses
- Bride + Groom + all grandparents
- [etc.]

I'll work through the list quickly and systematically. Without a list, formals take twice as long and we inevitably miss someone.

### Designate a Family Wrangler

This is the single most effective thing you can do to keep family formals moving. Choose one person on each side of the family — someone everyone listens to, someone who knows who's who — and ask them to help round people up. Give them a copy of the shot list. When they're calling people in, I can be ready to shoot immediately instead of waiting.

### Recommended Groupings and Order

Start with the largest groups and work down to smaller ones. This way, older guests and grandparents can be excused early (they've been standing long enough). Finish with the wedding party.

**Plan for approximately 3–4 minutes per grouping.** A list of 15 groupings = roughly 45 minutes.

### Keep It Moving

Once formals start, we keep them moving. This is not the time for conversation or catching up — there will be a reception for that. Get in position, smile, laugh, done. Your guests will thank you.

## Ceremony Considerations

### The Unplugged Ceremony

I strongly recommend asking your officiant to make an announcement — before the processional begins — asking guests to put their phones and cameras away and be fully present.

Here's why this matters photographically: guests leaning into the aisle to get a phone shot block my view at the exact moments I most need it — your processional, your vows, your first kiss. Phones and tablets create visual clutter in the background and in the aisle.

**Sample announcement:** "Before we begin, the couple has requested an unplugged ceremony. Please silence your phones and keep them put away so that everyone can be fully present. The photographer will make sure you have beautiful images to enjoy after the wedding."

Most guests are relieved — they get to actually *be* at your wedding instead of experiencing it through a screen.

### Where Immediate Family Should Sit

For the processional, parents and grandparents who will be walked down the aisle should begin in the back of the venue. I'll coordinate with them before the ceremony begins. Make sure they know to arrive 15–20 minutes early.

For the ceremony itself: **front rows, aisle seats are ideal for immediate family** who will have emotional reactions you'll want captured. Parents wiping tears, siblings beaming — these shots require them to be visible from where I'm positioned.

### Aisle Width and Lighting Notes

**Aisle width:** I need to be able to move freely on both sides of the aisle. If your venue has a very narrow aisle, let me know in advance so we can discuss positioning.

**Ceremony lighting:** If your ceremony is in a dimly lit church or venue with mixed artificial and natural light, please let me know as far in advance as possible. I will adjust my equipment accordingly, but I need to plan ahead for challenging light situations.

## Reception Coverage

### The Key Moments — and When to Schedule Them

**First dance:** Ideally one of the first events of the reception, when guests are energized and you're at your most glowing. Give me a heads-up about 5 minutes before so I'm in position.

**Parent dances:** Immediately following the first dance keeps the emotional momentum going.

**Toasts:** Schedule these after dinner when guests are settled but before they're tired. 3–4 toasts is ideal. Let your best man and maid of honor know ahead of time approximately how long is appropriate (2–3 minutes each is a good guideline).

**Cake cutting:** This is a great interlude between dinner and dancing. Let me know when you're planning to cut — I'll need 2–3 minutes to get in position.

**Bouquet/garter toss:** If you're including these, schedule them early in the dancing portion of the evening, when the dance floor is full and energy is high.

### Sparkler Exit or Send-Off Logistics

Sparkler exits are stunning in photos — but they require coordination.

- **Sparklers:** Use long sparklers (36-inch). Short ones burn out too fast.
- **Lighters:** Have multiple lighters going at once. Getting 50 sparklers lit takes longer than you think.
- **Form two lines:** Guests should form two parallel lines with enough space between them for you to walk through without being touched by a sparkler.
- **Walk slowly.** Seriously — slowly. Look at each other. Pause. Let me get the shot.
- **Designate a coordinator** to orchestrate the exit while you're inside waiting to come out.

Let me know your send-off plan at least a week before the wedding so we can coordinate timing.

### Reception in Low Light

Evening receptions in dimly lit venues are a creative challenge I love. Here's what to know:

- **I will use off-camera flash** for reception coverage. This is standard and will not be disruptive.
- **Dance floor lighting matters.** Colored party lights are beautiful and fun — but they can cast unexpected color on faces. DJ-provided uplighting in your wedding colors tends to photograph better than multicolored strobe effects.
- **If you want detail photos of the reception décor,** please make sure I have access to the room before guests are seated. Décor photographs best before the room fills up.

## What to Do the Night Before

### Lay Everything Out

The night before your wedding, gather all your detail items (see the list above) and put them in one place. Set out your rings, your invitation suite, your shoes, your jewelry. Do this now, not on the morning of, when your brain will be operating at approximately 40% capacity and you will not be able to find anything.

### Get a Good Night's Sleep — Photographically, It Matters

I know this sounds impossible. But here's the truth: eyes that are puffy from crying or a late night show up in photos. Skin that is rested and hydrated photographs differently than skin that is stressed and exhausted. You will look more beautiful if you sleep. Do your best.

**The night before, avoid:** excessive alcohol, very salty foods (puffiness), and staying up past midnight if you can help it.

**Drink a lot of water.** Start the day hydrated. It matters.

### Confirm Your Timeline

Send me a quick message the week of your wedding confirming our start time and any last-minute timeline changes. If anything has shifted — if your ceremony time moved, if you've decided to add a first look — tell me now so I can adjust my plan accordingly.

## Understanding Your Brand Identity for the Session

Before we touch your wardrobe or props, I want you to think clearly about your brand. The most effective brand photos are not just beautiful — they're *specific*. They speak to a particular person and convey a particular feeling.

Take 20 minutes and write out your answers to these questions:

**Who is your ideal client?**
Get specific. Not "small business owners" — but "female founders in their 30s and 40s who run service-based businesses and are ready to scale." The more specific you are, the more targeted your photos can be.

**What feeling should your brand convey?**
Pick three words. Not "professional" — that's table stakes. Think: *warm and approachable*, *polished and authoritative*, *creative and energetic*, *calm and expert*, *bold and direct*. These three words will guide every decision we make in the session.

**What colors, textures, and aesthetics represent you?**
Look at your existing branding — your website, your logo, your social media palette. Your photos should feel like an extension of that, not like a separate thing.

**Where will these photos primarily be used?**
Website headers, Instagram, LinkedIn, press features, speaking engagements? The use case affects the framing, the crops, and the variety of shots we need.

### Three Mood Board Examples

If you haven't built a mood board yet, here are three directions to consider. You don't have to pick one of these exactly — they're starting points.

**Direction 1: Warm and Approachable Professional**
Think: rich creams, warm taupes, terracotta, and soft greenery. Natural textures. Linen blazer. A coffee mug. A laugh that's genuine. This direction says: *I'm excellent at what I do, and I'm also a real person you'll actually enjoy working with.* Works beautifully for coaches, consultants, therapists, financial advisors, and anyone in a relationship-driven business.

**Direction 2: Clean, Modern, Minimal**
Think: white and off-white backgrounds, sharp lines, minimal props, strong eye contact, neutral wardrobe. Let the product or the person be the focus. This direction says: *I'm sharp, decisive, and credible.* Works beautifully for tech founders, attorneys, real estate agents, financial professionals, and anyone who wants their brand to feel crisp and authoritative.

**Direction 3: Creative and Bold**
Think: rich color, unexpected angles, dynamic poses, props that tell a story, personality-forward. This direction says: *I don't do things the conventional way — and that's exactly why you should hire me.* Works beautifully for designers, artists, marketers, brand strategists, photographers, and other creatives who want to stand out visually.

Build a Pinterest board with 10–15 images that represent the feeling you want. Share it with me at least one week before our session.

## Wardrobe Planning

### How Many Outfits to Bring

**Bring 3–5 complete outfits.** Here's why this matters:

First, variety in your gallery gives you more content to use across more contexts. A headshot for your LinkedIn looks different from a lifestyle photo for your homepage banner. Second, sometimes an outfit that looked perfect at home reads differently on camera — having options means you're never stuck.

Plan for the following:
- **1–2 "hero" outfits** — polished, on-brand, your best first impression
- **1–2 "lifestyle" outfits** — slightly more casual, movement-friendly, great for behind-the-scenes or desk scenes
- **1 bold or unexpected option** — something with more color or personality that might become a signature image

### Colors That Work With Your Brand

Bring your brand colors into your wardrobe intentionally. If your website is navy and gold, a navy blazer makes every photo feel cohesive with your digital presence. If your brand is earthy and warm, lean into terracotta, olive, and camel.

**Colors that photograph especially well:** Rich jewel tones (emerald, sapphire, burgundy), warm neutrals (camel, caramel, ivory), navy, and dusty rose. These all look beautiful on camera and hold their color well.

**Colors to approach with caution:** Very bright saturated colors (they can overwhelm the photo and cast light on skin), pure white in bright light (can blow out), and black in very dark environments (can go flat).

### What to Avoid

- **Busy patterns.** Thin stripes (especially horizontal) can create a moiré effect on camera — a visual vibration that's distracting and difficult to fix in editing. Small detailed prints can get visually busy. Keep patterns to one piece per outfit and make sure they're bold enough to be deliberate.
- **Brand logos or text on clothing.** Wearing another brand's logo in your brand photos is counterproductive.
- **Colors that clash with your existing brand palette.** Your photos need to live on your website and social feed. If they don't match your branding, they'll feel disconnected.

### Fit and Tailoring

This is not a place to save money. Clothes that fit well make you look and feel confident. Clothes that pull, bunch, or gap do the opposite — and that discomfort shows in your body language.

**Before the session:**
- Try on every outfit fully (including the shoes) and sit down, stand up, reach forward, and move around in it
- Identify anything that needs a last-minute repair or steaming
- Have a tailor take in anything that's close but not quite right

**Note on shapewear:** If you wear it in real life and it makes you feel more confident, wear it for the session. If you don't and it's uncomfortable, skip it. Comfort = confidence = better photos.

### Layers, Blazers, and Accessories

Layers add versatility. A blazer can come on and off, giving you two looks from one outfit. A lightweight cardigan over a simple tank does the same.

**Accessories to bring:**

- Earrings that vary in size and style
- A watch if you wear one
- A belt if it completes an outfit
- Glasses (if you wear them — bring your everyday pair and any others you own)

### Shoes Matter Even When They're Not in Every Shot

Full-length shots will show your shoes. And more importantly: wearing the right shoes affects how you stand, how you move, and how you feel. Don't show up to a polished brand session in your commute sneakers.

Bring the shoes that complete each outfit. Keep them clean. And if there's a chance we'll do any outdoor shooting, make sure they're appropriate for the surface.

## Expression and Posing Prep

### Practice in the Mirror (Seriously — Do This)

I know it sounds awkward. It's not. Here's what to practice:

- **A relaxed, genuine smile.** Not a forced "say cheese" smile — but the smile you have when someone says something that actually makes you happy. Think about what that is. Practice the feeling, not the expression.
- **A serious, focused look.** Eyes sharp, jaw slightly relaxed, thinking. This is the "credibility shot" — the one that goes on the back of your book or on the press page of your website.
- **A genuine laugh.** Think of something actually funny. Practice letting it come out. These photos — the unexpected, real-laugh shots — are almost always the most popular.

You don't need to memorize poses. You need to get your face used to the idea of being looked at. Ten minutes in front of a mirror does more than you'd think.

Get a good night's sleep the night before. Eat a real breakfast or lunch before you arrive. If you need a coffee, have one. Show up ready to bring your best self, because that's the self we're here to photograph.

### The Photographer's Role in Directing You

I will guide you throughout the entire session. You do not need to know how to pose. You do not need to perform. I will tell you where to stand, how to hold your arms, when to look at the camera and when to look away. My job is to watch for the moments when you look most like yourself — and to capture them.

The only thing I need from you is to stay present, stay loose, and trust the process.

## Choosing Your Location

The location of your engagement session shapes everything — the mood, the colors, the story your images tell. Let's choose it thoughtfully.

### How to Think About Location

The best locations are three things at once: **meaningful** (connected to your relationship), **beautiful** (visually interesting and flattering as a backdrop), and **practical** (accessible, with good light, photographically workable).

You don't have to find all three in equal measure. A location that's incredibly meaningful to your relationship but not "Instagram-famous" will often produce the most authentic, personal photos. Think about where you went on your first date, where they proposed, a neighborhood that feels like *you*.

### Five Location Types: Pros and Cons

**1. Urban / City**

*What it offers:* Architecture, texture, color, graphic shapes. The sense of a real life being lived. Great for couples who are city people at heart.

*The pros:* Lots of visual variety in a small area. Easy to move between locations. Works in any season.

*The cons:* Midday urban settings can be harsh-lit and busy. We'll want to shoot at golden hour or in shaded pockets of the city. Foot traffic can be a challenge.

*Best for:* Couples who met in the city, live in the city, or whose aesthetic is more modern and editorial than natural and organic.

**2. Park or Nature**

*What it offers:* Trees, paths, open fields, water features — soft, organic, timeless backdrops.

*The pros:* Light filters beautifully through trees. Green and natural tones are universally flattering. Lots of room to move.

*The cons:* Popular parks can be crowded. Trails can be muddy or uneven.

*Best for:* Couples who love the outdoors, want a timeless feel, or whose relationship has a lot of outdoor memories.

**3. Beach**

*What it offers:* Expansive light, the sound of waves, dramatic sky and water, golden sand.

*The pros:* Beach light at sunset is otherworldly. The sound and movement of the ocean creates a natural, relaxed energy.

*The cons:* Wind is almost always a factor (be ready for it — or embrace it). Sand gets into everything. Strong midday beach light is harsh and unflattering; this location requires a sunset timing.

*Best for:* Couples who love the water, who want romantic and dramatic images, who are getting married on or near the coast.

**4. Golden Hour Field or Open Landscape**

*What it offers:* Wide open sky, sweeping light, wild grasses, a sense of vastness.

*The pros:* Golden hour fields are genuinely magical. The light does half the work. Beautiful for romantic, editorial, dreamy imagery.

*The cons:* Requires precise timing — golden hour is short. May require advance scouting. Footing can be uneven.

*Best for:* Couples who want their engagement photos to feel cinematic and romantic. Worth the planning.

**5. Historic Building or Architecture**

*What it offers:* Beautiful structural detail, columns, arches, aged materials, a sense of permanence.

*The pros:* These locations often have beautiful interior light. The architecture provides built-in visual interest.

*The cons:* Some historic venues require permits for photography. May have restricted access.

*Best for:* Couples who love history and architecture, who want something more formal and classical, or who want photos that feel timeless rather than current-trend.

### A Note on Scouting

I will scout your location in advance to identify the best spots, map out our movement, and note where the light will fall at your session time. If you've seen a specific spot you want to include — a bench where you had your first conversation, an alley you love — send me the details and I'll incorporate it.

## What to Wear

### The Case for Two Outfits

Two outfits give you two different stories from one session. I recommend one that's a little more dressed up and one that's more casual — think "dinner and a walk through the city" versus "wandering through the park on a Sunday."

Having a wardrobe change also gives the session a natural pause point, which can help you reset your energy and try something different with your vibe.

**My recommendation:** Pack your second outfit in a small bag you leave with me or in the car. A quick change takes 5 minutes and doubles your variety.

### Coordinating Without Matching

Two people in identical outfits look like costumes. Two people who've chosen from a shared color palette look like a couple. That's the goal.

**How to coordinate:** Pick 2–3 anchor colors and each build your outfit around them. One person in a dusty blue linen shirt; the other in a cream dress with a dusty blue accessory. Same family, different expression.

Talk through your outfits ahead of time and lay them next to each other. If they look like they belong in the same photo, you're set.

### Colors That Photograph Well Outdoors

**Excellent choices:** Warm neutrals (cream, camel, ivory, tan), rich earth tones (terracotta, olive, rust, burgundy), muted pastels (dusty pink, sage, lavender, dusty blue), and deep jewel tones (navy, emerald, wine).

**Why these work:** They complement natural settings — green trees, warm skies, sandy beaches — rather than fighting them. They also hold up well in varying light conditions.

**Handle with care:** Very bright colors and neon shades can overwhelm the photo and make it feel dated quickly. Bold color can work, but it should be one piece, not the whole outfit.

### Textures for Visual Interest

Flat, solid-colored fabrics can look a little lifeless in photos. Textures — linen, denim, cable-knit, lace, chambray, velvet, suede — add dimension and visual richness.

If both of you are in solid colors, look for at least one piece with texture or subtle pattern to add depth.

### What NOT to Wear

- **Logos and sports jerseys.** These don't age well in photos and pull the eye to the brand rather than to you.
- **Neon or fluorescent colors.** They cast light on skin and compete with the landscape.
- **All-black head to toe.** Black is great as an element, but an entirely black outfit can go flat in photos, especially in shaded settings. If you love black, balance it with a pop of another color or a rich texture.
- **Matching couple outfits.** I know — they look cute in theory. In photos, they read as costumes.

### Footwear for Every Setting

**Outdoor/nature settings:** Wear something you can actually walk in. Beautiful boots, clean white sneakers, or simple sandals all work. Stilettos on a hiking trail do not.

**Urban settings:** More latitude here. Heels work on pavement. Loafers, oxfords, and ankle boots look clean and intentional.

**Beach sessions:** Barefoot is beautiful. If you want shoes in some shots, simple sandals in a neutral color.

**General rule:** Whatever the setting, shoes should be clean, intentional, and comfortable enough that you're not thinking about them during the session.

### One Specific Request: Clean Your Ring

Your engagement ring will be in many of these photos. Give it a clean the day before your session — either professionally or at home with a gentle toothbrush and warm soapy water. A clean ring catches light beautifully. A smudged one photographs flat. This takes 5 minutes and makes a visible difference.

## Posing Tips to Share Before the Session

### Don't Worry About Posing — I'll Guide You

If the idea of being photographed makes you nervous, you're in the majority. Most people are not comfortable in front of a camera. You do not need to be. That is entirely my job.

From the moment we start, I will tell you exactly what to do — where to put your hands, which direction to look, when to walk, when to stop. You will never be standing in front of me wondering what to do with your bodies. I promise.

### What to Expect

Here are some of the types of shots and moments we'll create together. None of them require acting or performance — just presence.

**The walking shot:** I'll have you walk toward me or past me, talking to each other, holding hands. It sounds simple because it is. It's one of the most natural and beautiful setups in engagement photography because movement breaks the stiffness of "posing."

**The candid laugh:** I'll say something, or I'll tell you to say something ridiculous to each other. A genuine, surprised laugh is worth a hundred posed smiles.

**The forehead touch:** A quiet moment — stand close, touch foreheads, close your eyes, breathe. It's intimate and still and deeply romantic.

**The walking-away shot:** You walk away from me together, hand in hand. You don't need to look back (though sometimes you will and it'll be perfect).

**The candid moment:** I'll step back and just let you be. Talk to each other. Be together. Forget I'm there for a minute. These are often the most powerful images of a session.

### How to Feel Natural

Movement is your friend. When people stand still and "pose," they tend to stiffen. When I keep you moving — walking, turning, sitting down, getting up — you stay loose and natural.

Don't try to look at the camera and "smile" in every shot. Some of the best engagement images have neither person looking at the camera. Trust me to watch for the moments when you look most like yourselves.

### The Engagement Ring Shot

We will absolutely get ring shots. Here's how to set them up beautifully:

- **Hands should be relaxed and soft, not flat.** Slightly curved fingers look natural; stiff flat hands look awkward.
- **Good lighting is key.** I'll find it — just trust my direction on hand placement.
- **Include both hands in some shots** — your hands stacked together, both rings visible, tells a complete story.
