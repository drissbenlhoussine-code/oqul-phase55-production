# Guide 02: Client Tracker & Inventory Manager
## How to Build a Simple CRM and Manage Your Stock

---

## Overview

Two of the most common pain points for small business owners are losing track of client relationships and running out of stock at the worst possible moment. The Client Tracker and Inventory Manager in this toolkit are purpose-built to solve both problems. Whether you manage a handful of high-value consulting clients or a growing product-based shop with dozens of SKUs, these tools give you the structure to stay organized, professional, and in control — without the complexity or cost of enterprise software.

---

## Part 1: Client Tracker / CRM (04_Client_Tracker_CRM.csv)

### What It Is

The Client Tracker is a lightweight Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system designed for small business owners who need to manage client relationships without the overhead of complex software like Salesforce or HubSpot. It gives you a single, organized view of every lead, prospect, and active client — including their contact information, project status, budget, follow-up dates, and key notes. When used consistently, it ensures that no potential client is forgotten, no follow-up is missed, and no active project falls through the cracks.

### How to Set It Up

1. **Open the file in Google Sheets** by uploading the CSV to Google Drive and selecting Open With > Google Sheets. Save it as a native Google Sheet.
2. **Freeze the header row** (View > Freeze > 1 Row) so your column labels stay visible as your client list grows.
3. **Review the existing column structure.** The default columns cover: Client Name, Business Name, Contact Email, Phone, Lead Source, Status, Service/Product, Project Value, Start Date, Deadline, Follow-Up Date, and Notes. Customize these columns to match your specific business model.
4. **Add your current clients first.** Start by entering every active client and ongoing project. Fill in as much detail as possible for each entry — the more complete your records, the more useful the tracker becomes.
5. **Then add your leads and prospects.** Any business contact you've spoken to, quoted, or are in conversation with belongs in this tracker under the appropriate status.
6. **Sort or filter by status** to quickly see which clients are active, which leads need follow-up, and which projects are approaching their deadlines.

### How to Use It — Step by Step

1. **Log every new inquiry immediately.** The moment someone expresses interest in your services — whether via email, DM, or a discovery call — create an entry in your Client Tracker. Set their status to "Lead" and record the date and source of contact.
2. **Update the status after every interaction.** As clients move through your process — from Lead to Proposal Sent to Active to Completed — update their status in the tracker in real time.
3. **Set a follow-up date for every open record.** Any lead or prospect who hasn't converted yet should have a concrete follow-up date in the Follow-Up Date column. Review this column every Monday morning and take action on any entries dated today or earlier.
4. **Add detailed notes after every client conversation.** Record the key points of every call, email, or meeting. Include what the client needs, what was promised, any objections raised, and what the next step is. These notes become invaluable context when you circle back weeks later.
5. **Record project values for every client.** Tracking the monetary value associated with each client lets you prioritize your time toward your highest-value relationships and gives you data for calculating your average client value over time.
6. **Archive completed clients — don't delete them.** When a project is finished, change the status to "Completed" and move the row to an "Archive" tab rather than deleting it. Past clients are your warmest future leads.
7. **Review the full tracker weekly.** Spend 10–15 minutes every Monday reviewing all active leads and clients, confirming follow-up dates are set, and ensuring no record has been stagnant for more than 2 weeks without a reason.

### Understanding Client Statuses

Use consistent status labels to keep your CRM meaningful and filterable:

- **Lead:** A new inquiry or contact who has expressed initial interest but hasn't received a proposal yet.
- **Proposal Sent:** You've sent a quote or proposal and are awaiting a response.
- **Negotiating:** You're in active discussion about scope, pricing, or terms.
- **Active:** The client has confirmed and paid; a project is underway.
- **Awaiting Payment:** Work is complete or partially complete, and you're waiting on payment.
- **Completed:** The project has been delivered and paid in full. All obligations are met.
- **On Hold:** The project is paused at the client's request. Set a follow-up date to check in.
- **Lost:** The prospect chose not to move forward. Add a brief note on why, if known. This data helps you improve your sales process.

### Building a Follow-Up System

Consistent follow-up is one of the most underutilized growth levers for small businesses. Most prospects don't say no immediately — they say "not yet." A structured follow-up system ensures you stay top of mind without being pushy.

- Set a follow-up rule: any unanswered proposal gets a follow-up at 3 days, 7 days, and 14 days after sending.
- Use the Notes column to track every touchpoint so you know exactly when you last reached out and what you said.
- When you follow up, add value — send a relevant case study, a helpful tip, or an update about your services rather than simply asking "have you decided yet?"
- Review your "Lost" clients quarterly. Circumstances change — a prospect who said no six months ago may be ready now.

### Growing Client Relationships

The best way to grow your business is to deepen the relationships you already have. Use your Client Tracker as a relationship intelligence tool, not just a project management system:

- Add personal details in the Notes column — the client's business anniversary, a project milestone they mentioned, or a challenge they're working through. Reference these in future conversations to show you pay attention.
- Schedule a "client appreciation" outreach each quarter — a brief check-in email to past clients to see how things are going. No sales pitch, just genuine relationship maintenance.
- Track which clients refer the most business to you. These are your VIP relationships and deserve extra attention and gratitude.
- Use the tracker to identify upsell opportunities — clients who completed one project may be ready for another if their needs have evolved.

### Example Use Case

Marcus runs a freelance social media management agency. He uses his Client Tracker to manage 12 active clients and a rotating pipeline of 8–15 leads at any given time. Every Monday, he filters the tracker by Follow-Up Date and sees that 3 prospects are due for contact. He sends each one a personalized email referencing the last conversation he noted in the tracker. One of those prospects converts to an active client that same week. By reviewing his "Source" column quarterly, Marcus has discovered that 70% of his best clients come from Instagram referrals — so he's doubled his investment of time on Instagram and seen his lead flow grow accordingly.

## Part 2: Inventory Tracker (06_Inventory_Tracker.csv)

### What It Is

The Inventory Tracker is designed for product-based businesses — whether you sell physical goods through an online shop, at markets, or to wholesale buyers — to monitor stock levels, track costs, manage suppliers, and identify when it's time to reorder. It gives you a clear, real-time view of what you have, what it cost you, what it's worth at retail, and what your profit margin is on each product. For service businesses that sell any physical materials or supplies, it also works as a supply management tool to ensure you never run short on what you need to deliver your services.

### How to Set It Up

1. **Upload the CSV to Google Drive** and open it with Google Sheets. Save as a native Google Sheet.
2. **Create one row per unique product or SKU.** A SKU (Stock Keeping Unit) is a unique identifier you assign to each distinct product — for example, SKU-001, or a more descriptive code like PLNR-MNT-BLU for a blue monthly planner.
3. **Enter your opening inventory counts.** Record how many units of each product you currently have on hand in the "Current Stock" column.
4. **Fill in cost, price, and supplier information** for every product. Accurate cost and price data is essential for calculating your profit margin per item.
5. **Set your reorder point for each product.** The reorder point is the stock level at which you need to place a new order to avoid running out. For a product that takes 2 weeks to restock and sells 10 units per week, your reorder point should be at least 20 units.
6. **Update stock counts after every sale, return, or new stock receipt.** The tracker is only useful if it reflects your actual inventory in real time.

### How to Use It — Step by Step

1. **After every sale**, subtract the quantity sold from the Current Stock column for the relevant product(s). If you sell through a platform like Shopify or Etsy, do this at the end of each day or connect a manual sync routine.
2. **After receiving a new shipment**, add the received quantity to your Current Stock and update the Last Restocked date.
3. **Check for items at or below the reorder point** each week. Any product at or below its reorder point should be ordered immediately to prevent stockouts.
4. **Update costs whenever supplier pricing changes.** Supplier price increases directly impact your profit margin — update your tracker and recalculate your retail pricing if needed.
5. **Track slow-moving inventory.** Products that haven't sold in 60+ days may need to be discounted, bundled with faster sellers, or discontinued.
6. **Use the profit margin column** to identify your most and least profitable products. Focus your marketing energy on promoting your highest-margin items.

### Understanding SKUs

A SKU is a unique code that identifies a specific product in your inventory. Clear, consistent SKU naming makes tracking much easier, especially as your product range grows.

A good SKU structure includes:
- **Product category code:** e.g., PLNR (Planner), PRNT (Printable), SUPP (Supply)
- **Product identifier:** e.g., MNT (Monthly), WKY (Weekly), FLR (Floral)
- **Variation code:** e.g., BLU (Blue), RED (Red), LRG (Large), SM (Small)
- **Sequence number:** 001, 002, 003

Example: PLNR-MNT-BLU-001 = Monthly Planner, Blue, First Version

Avoid using supplier part numbers as your SKUs, as they will change if you switch suppliers. Create and own your own SKU system from the start.

### Setting Reorder Alerts

Your reorder point should account for:
- **Lead time:** How many days does it take to receive stock after placing an order?
- **Sales velocity:** How many units do you sell per day or per week on average?
- **Safety stock:** A buffer quantity to account for unexpected demand spikes or supplier delays (typically 1–2 weeks of average sales)

**Reorder Point Formula:** (Average Daily Sales × Lead Time in Days) + Safety Stock

For example, if you sell an average of 5 units per day and your supplier takes 10 days to deliver, with a safety stock of 10 units: Reorder Point = (5 × 10) + 10 = 60 units.

Set a weekly reminder to check all items against their reorder points, or use conditional formatting in Google Sheets to automatically highlight any row where Current Stock is below the Reorder Point.

### Managing Suppliers

Use the supplier columns in your Inventory Tracker to maintain all supplier information in one place:

- **Supplier Name and Contact:** Keep this updated so you can reorder quickly without hunting for contact details.
- **Lead Time:** Track how long each supplier typically takes to deliver. Use this in your reorder point calculations.
- **Minimum Order Quantity (MOQ):** Some suppliers require a minimum order. Note this so you're never caught short when calculating reorder amounts.
- **Supplier Notes:** Record payment terms, quality issues, special discounts, or any other relevant context.

Review your supplier relationships quarterly. Are there alternative suppliers who offer better pricing, faster lead times, or higher quality? Building a relationship with at least one backup supplier for your key products protects you from supply disruptions.

### Profit Analysis by Product

The Inventory Tracker includes columns for your Unit Cost and Retail Price, which allow you to calculate your gross profit margin per product:

**Gross Margin % = ((Retail Price − Unit Cost) ÷ Retail Price) × 100**

For a product that costs $4.50 to produce and sells for $14.99, your gross margin is: (($14.99 − $4.50) ÷ $14.99) × 100 = **70%**

Use this data to:
- Prioritize your marketing spend toward your highest-margin products
- Identify products with margins too thin to sustain (typically anything below 40% for physical goods)
- Negotiate better pricing with suppliers for low-margin but high-volume items
- Make informed decisions about whether to discount slow-moving inventory

### Example Use Case

Priya sells handmade candles online and at local craft markets. She uses her Inventory Tracker to manage 18 SKUs across three product lines. She has set reorder points for all her key supplies — wax, wicks, fragrance oils, and containers — based on her average weekly sales volume. Before the holiday season, she notices that her Cinnamon Spice candle is selling 3x faster than normal. Because her tracker has an automatic highlight (via conditional formatting) when any product falls below its reorder point, she catches the alert early and places a supplier order with enough lead time to restock before a market event. She avoids a stockout that would have cost her an estimated $600 in lost sales. She also uses the profit margin column to discover that her soy candle line generates 72% gross margin versus 51% for her beeswax line — information she uses to decide which products to feature most prominently at her next market.
