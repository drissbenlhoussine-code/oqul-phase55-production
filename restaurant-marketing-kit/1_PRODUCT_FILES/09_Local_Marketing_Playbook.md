# Local Marketing & Community Growth Playbook

## How to Use This Playbook

This playbook is not about going viral. It is not about chasing trends, gaming algorithms, or spending money on ads before you have built a foundation. It is about something more durable and more profitable: becoming the default restaurant in your neighborhood. The default restaurant is the one your neighbors recommend without being asked. It is the place people drive past and say "that's our spot." It is the restaurant that shows up at the top of local search results, that has a hundred genuine reviews, and that fills tables on a Tuesday night because its regulars come back on their own schedule. That kind of presence is not built overnight, and it is not built with a single viral moment. It is built through the daily accumulation of small, consistent actions — posting when it feels like nobody is watching, asking one more guest for a review, walking into the florist next door and introducing yourself. This playbook gives you those actions in a structured, sequenced system.

Every section of this playbook works independently, but the results compound when you work through them together. Start with Section 1 and treat your Google Business Profile as a living asset, not a one-time setup task. Layer in email collection (Section 3) and local SEO habits (Section 2) in the first month. Then expand outward into creator relationships (Section 4) and neighborhood partnerships (Section 5) once your foundation is solid. The 90-day plan in Section 7 shows you exactly when to do what. Come back to this document every quarter, because local marketing is not a campaign — it is a practice. The restaurants that win in their neighborhoods are the ones still doing these things twelve months from now.

## SECTION 1 — GOOGLE BUSINESS PROFILE OPTIMIZATION

### Complete Setup Checklist

Your Google Business Profile (GBP) is the single most important free marketing asset available to an independent restaurant. When someone searches "restaurants near me" or "brunch in [your neighborhood]," your GBP listing is typically the first thing they see — before your website, before Yelp, before Instagram. Every field matters. Work through this checklist once and then revisit it every quarter to keep it current.

- [ ] **Business name:** Use your exact legal operating name as it appears on your storefront signage. Do not add keywords or descriptors (e.g., "Mario's Pizzeria — Best Pizza in Denver" is a policy violation and will be flagged). Just "Mario's Pizzeria."
- [ ] **Primary category:** Select the most specific option available. "Italian Restaurant" beats "Restaurant." "Sushi Restaurant" beats "Japanese Restaurant" if sushi is your core offering. Your primary category has the largest impact on which searches you appear in.
- [ ] **Secondary categories:** Add up to 9 additional relevant categories. Examples: "Pizza Restaurant," "Wine Bar," "Seafood Restaurant," "Cocktail Bar," "Catering Food and Drink Supplier," "Brunch Restaurant," "Takeout Restaurant." Every relevant secondary category is an additional search query you can appear in.
- [ ] **Address:** Enter the exact address that appears on your physical signage and matches what is on your website. Do not abbreviate differently here than elsewhere (see Section 2 on NAP consistency).
- [ ] **Phone number:** Use your primary local phone number with area code. Avoid using a call tracking number that differs from your publicly displayed number — inconsistency hurts your local SEO.
- [ ] **Website URL:** Link to your homepage or your direct reservations page if it is more useful. Update this link any time your website changes.
- [ ] **Regular hours:** Set accurate open and close times for every day of the week. If you close between lunch and dinner, set split hours (e.g., Mon-Fri 11am-2pm and 5pm-10pm).
- [ ] **Special/holiday hours:** Set hours in advance for every major holiday — New Year's Eve, New Year's Day, Valentine's Day, Mother's Day, Memorial Day, Fourth of July, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve, Christmas Day. Google will prompt guests with a banner when a holiday is approaching. If your hours are not set, guests see "hours may differ" which reduces confidence.
- [ ] **Business description (750 characters):** Write a keyword-rich description using the following structure — what you are, where you are, what you serve, what makes you special, what occasions you serve best. Include: your cuisine type, your neighborhood name, your city, 2-3 signature dishes by name, and 1-2 occasion or atmosphere descriptors ("perfect for date night," "ideal for family celebrations," "great for business lunches"). Example: "Rosario's is a family-owned Italian restaurant in Chicago's Andersonville neighborhood. We serve hand-rolled pasta, wood-fired Neapolitan pizza, and seasonal dishes made from locally sourced ingredients. Open since 2008, we've become a neighborhood staple for romantic dinners, birthday celebrations, and regular weeknight dining. Come in for our house-made lasagna, the wood-fired margherita, or our rotating seasonal risotto."
- [ ] **Menu link:** Add your direct menu URL in the "Menu" field — link to your actual menu page, not just the homepage.
- [ ] **Booking link:** Connect OpenTable, Resy, Tock, SevenRooms, or your direct online reservation form in the "Reservations" field. This feeds the "Reserve a table" button that appears on your listing.
- [ ] **Products/services section:** Use this section to list your main dishes, signature drinks, or service offerings. Each entry can have a name, description, and price. This helps you appear in dish-specific searches like "best duck confit [city]."
- [ ] **Q&A section:** Pre-seed at least 5 questions and answers yourself (see subsection below).
- [ ] **Attributes:** Check every applicable attribute from the list. Key ones: Dine-in, Takeout, Delivery, Outdoor seating, Wheelchair accessible, Accepts credit cards, Accepts debit cards, Free Wi-Fi, Good for kids, Kids' menu, Happy-hour drinks, Happy-hour food, Bar on-site, Dinner, Lunch, Brunch, Serves beer, Serves cocktails, Serves wine, Serves spirits, LGBTQ+ friendly. Each attribute is a potential search filter.
- [ ] **Messaging:** Enable the messaging feature and write an auto-reply (see subsection below).
- [ ] **Google Posts:** Schedule your first post before you finish this setup session (see subsection below).
- [ ] **Photos:** Upload a minimum of the required photos across all categories (see Photo Requirements subsection).
- [ ] **Verify your listing:** Confirm the green "Verified" badge appears in your dashboard. If not, initiate or re-initiate verification.

### Getting Your Review Link

Your Google review link is a direct URL that sends customers straight to the "Write a review" dialog for your business — no searching required. This link is one of the highest-value assets in your local marketing toolkit. Here is how to get it:

1. Go to **business.google.com** and sign in.
2. Click on your business profile from the dashboard.
3. In the left navigation, click **"Get more reviews."**
4. Google will display your unique review link. It will look something like: `https://g.page/r/[your-business-code]/review`
5. Click "Copy" to copy the full link to your clipboard.

Once you have the link, shorten it so it is easy to share and fits on printed materials. Go to **bit.ly**, create a free account, paste in the long Google link, and create a custom short link such as `bit.ly/RosariosReview`. This is much easier to say aloud and fits on a receipt footer.

To create a QR code version: go to **qr.io** or **canva.com/qr-code-generator**, paste in the full Google review URL, and download a high-resolution PNG. This QR code should go on your table tent cards, in your check presenter, on your receipt footer, and anywhere else you want to prompt reviews.

Share the link with every staff member: put it in a group text, post it in your staff communication channel, and write it on a card in the back of house. Staff should know the link exists and feel comfortable sending it to guests via text after an exceptional meal experience. Train your team to say: "If you'd like to leave us a review, I can text you the direct link — it takes about 30 seconds." Most guests who had a great experience are happy to do it when it is that easy.

### Photo Requirements

Profiles with more photos receive significantly more clicks and direction requests than profiles with fewer photos. Google's own data shows that listings with more than 100 photos get dramatically more engagement. Treat your GBP photo gallery like a visual menu — it is often the deciding factor between a guest choosing you or your competitor.

**Interior shots (minimum 10):** Photograph the full dining room, showing the layout and atmosphere at different times. Include the bar area if you have one. Include a private dining room or semi-private area if you have one. Capture both the energy of a busy service — people at tables, candles lit, a sense of life — and the quiet warmth of your daytime setup. Guests want to know what they are walking into. Take photos with tables set for service, not empty and bare.

**Food photos (minimum 20):** Photograph every dish on your current menu, plus cocktails, desserts, and the bread or amuse-bouche if you offer them. Focus on your 5-6 hero dishes — the ones guests order most and the ones you are most proud of. Rotate seasonal dishes in and out as your menu changes. Use natural light whenever possible: position dishes near a window during daylight hours. Avoid heavy filters. Do not use the word "food" as a photo label — give each photo a descriptive caption using relevant keywords (e.g., "House-made tagliatelle with braised short rib and truffle oil").

**Team photos (minimum 5):** Show the people behind the food. Include the chef or chefs plating a dish, front-of-house staff smiling at the host stand, bartenders building cocktails, and candid shots from service. Human faces build trust in ways food photos alone cannot. Guests feel like they know you before they walk in the door.

**Exterior photos (minimum 3):** Shoot from the street to show exactly what guests will see when they arrive. Include at least one daytime shot and one evening shot showing your signage lit up or your patio glowing. If you have a patio, photograph it separately. If your entrance is hard to find, photograph what it looks like from the street with a caption like "Look for the red door on Oak Street."

**Event photos:** As you host events — wine dinners, live music nights, private parties, holiday specials — photograph them and upload them to GBP. Events show your restaurant as an active, living place rather than a static listing.

**Upload frequency:** Add at least 2 new photos per week. Set a recurring weekly reminder to upload. Google's algorithm favors active profiles, and consistent photo uploads signal that your business is current and engaged.

**Photo quality requirements:** Minimum 720 pixels on the shortest side; ideally 1080 x 1080 or higher. Use JPG or PNG format. Horizontal (landscape) orientation is preferred for interior and exterior shots; square format works for food close-ups. No promotional text overlaid on photos. No watermarks. No heavy Instagram-style filters — Google's algorithm can deprioritize heavily edited images, and guests want to see what the food actually looks like.

### Posting Frequency

Google Business Profile posts appear directly on your search listing in the "Updates" section and the "From the Owner" tab. Standard posts expire after 7 days, which means you need to post at minimum once per week to maintain a visible presence. Two to three posts per week is ideal and will meaningfully improve your local search performance over time.

**Post types to use:**

- **What's New posts:** Weekly specials, new menu items, seasonal launches, staff spotlights. These are your workhorse post type — use them every week.
- **Offer posts:** Limited-time promotions, happy hour specials, early-bird deals. These show a "View offer" button prominently on your listing.
- **Event posts:** Any event with a start and end date — wine dinners, live music nights, prix fixe evenings, tasting menus. Event posts show up on your listing with the event dates displayed.

**Time-saving tip — repurpose your Instagram captions in under 5 minutes:** After you write an Instagram caption, paste it into a Google Post with the same photo. Expand it slightly (GBP allows up to 1,500 characters; Instagram caps at 2,200 but most people only read the first 125), add your city or neighborhood name if it is not already in the caption, and hit publish. This doubles your reach without doubling your workload. Block 15 minutes on Monday mornings to do both at once.

**Why GBP posts improve local search ranking:** Google treats your GBP profile as an active signal of business legitimacy. Profiles that post regularly show Google — and guests — that the business is active, current, and engaged. Consistent posting is one of the most reliable free tactics for improving where you appear in the local 3-pack (the map listing that shows three businesses at the top of local search results).

## SECTION 2 — LOCAL SEO BASICS FOR RESTAURANTS

### Keywords to Use in Your Bio and Posts

Local SEO for restaurants is simpler than it sounds. You are not trying to rank for "restaurant" nationally. You are trying to appear when someone in your city or neighborhood searches for exactly what you offer. The way you do that is by consistently using location-specific keywords wherever your restaurant appears online — your Google description, your Instagram bio, your post captions, and your website.

**Keyword strategy 1 — City + cuisine type:** The foundational local SEO keyword. "[City] Italian restaurant," "[City] sushi restaurant," "[City] brunch spot." Use this exact phrasing (not a variation) at least once in your Google description, once in your Instagram bio, and in the first line of your captions at least twice per month.

**Keyword strategy 2 — Neighborhood + dining:** More specific than city-level keywords, and often less competitive. "[Neighborhood] dining," "[Neighborhood] restaurant," "restaurants in [Neighborhood]." Guests searching at the neighborhood level are often very close to a decision — they know the area, they want something nearby. Example: "Logan Square dining," "Capitol Hill restaurant," "South End Boston."

**Keyword strategy 3 — Best [dish] in [city]:** Dish-specific searches are high-intent and often easier to rank for than broad cuisine terms. "Best tacos in Austin," "best ramen in Portland," "best oysters in New Orleans." Use these in captions when you post that dish, in your Google description, and on your website's dish pages if you have them.

**Keyword strategy 4 — Occasion + location:** Guests searching for occasion-based dining are motivated and ready to book. "Date night restaurant [city]," "family-friendly restaurant [neighborhood]," "private dining [city]," "anniversary dinner [city]," "birthday restaurant [neighborhood]." Include at least two occasion keywords in your Google description and work them into monthly post captions.

**Keyword strategy 5 — [Cuisine] in [city] + unique descriptor:** Differentiate within your cuisine category. "Farm-to-table Italian in Denver," "authentic Sichuan in Chicago," "wood-fired Neapolitan in Brooklyn." The unique descriptor helps you appear for guests specifically seeking what makes you different.

**Keyword strategy 6 — Dietary option + location:** As more guests filter by dietary need, these searches are growing. "Vegan restaurant [city]," "gluten-free friendly [neighborhood]," "plant-based dining [city]," "halal restaurant [neighborhood]." If you genuinely accommodate these needs well, use the language.

**Keyword strategy 7 — Brunch [neighborhood]:** Brunch is one of the highest-search-volume restaurant keywords in most markets. If you serve brunch, this deserves its own strategy. "Brunch [neighborhood]," "weekend brunch [city]," "Saturday brunch [neighborhood]," "[city] brunch spots." Brunch posts consistently outperform dinner posts on engagement — lean into it.

**Keyword strategy 8 — Near [landmark]:** Proximity to a well-known landmark or destination is a powerful local SEO signal. "Restaurant near [museum]," "dinner near [stadium]," "lunch near [hotel district]." Use this language in your Google description ("just two blocks from the Museum of Fine Arts") and in captions when relevant.

**Where to place these keywords naturally:** Your Google Business Profile description (the most important placement), your Instagram bio (include your city, neighborhood, and one cuisine keyword), the first line of your Instagram captions (Google indexes caption text), your website's homepage meta description and title tag, your Yelp and TripAdvisor profile descriptions, and the alt text on food photos on your website.

### Mentioning Your Neighborhood Naturally

Working location into your content is a habit, not a strategy. The goal is to mention your neighborhood or city often enough that Google's algorithm — and your human readers — associate you with that place, without making it sound forced or robotic.

Here are five specific caption phrases you can adapt to your restaurant:

1. "Bringing the best of [Neighborhood] to your table every night — come see what we have on the menu this week."
2. "Our little corner of [City] just got a whole lot tastier. New seasonal menu starts this Friday."
3. "Just down the street from [Landmark] and a world away from ordinary. Reservations open for the weekend."
4. "There's something about a [Neighborhood] evening that just calls for good food and good company. We'll be here."
5. "We've been feeding [City] since [year]. Tonight we're pouring something special — stop in and ask your server what it is."

These phrases feel like something a real person would write, not a keyword list. That is the goal. Authenticity and specificity travel together. When you mention the specific street, the nearby park, the local event happening this weekend, or the neighborhood coffee shop across the alley, you signal genuine community membership — and Google reads those signals.

**Geo-tagging matters as much as your caption text:** Every Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok post should be geo-tagged to your restaurant's specific location. This is separate from hashtags and separate from the caption. When you geo-tag consistently, your restaurant's location becomes associated with a growing body of content. Users browsing the location tag find you. The platforms index location-tagged content differently than untagged content. Make it a non-negotiable part of every post.

### Citation Building Basics

A citation is any online mention of your restaurant's Name, Address, and Phone number — referred to collectively as NAP. When Google finds your NAP consistently across many reputable websites, it treats your business as established, legitimate, and locally relevant — which directly improves where you appear in local search results. When it finds inconsistencies, it loses confidence in your listing.

**The core rule:** Your NAP must match exactly everywhere. Not "close enough" — exactly. "1234 North Main Street" and "1234 N. Main St." are inconsistent. "The Rosewood Grill" and "Rosewood Grill" are inconsistent. "555-867-5309" and "(555) 867-5309" are inconsistent in format even if numerically the same. Choose one format for each element and use it identically across every platform.

**Why inconsistency hurts rankings:** Google's local ranking algorithm uses citation consistency as a trust signal. When it finds your address listed three different ways across the web, it cannot confidently confirm which is accurate. That uncertainty lowers your ranking. Fixing inconsistencies is often the single highest-impact free action a restaurant can take to improve local search visibility.

**The 5 most important directories to claim and complete:**

1. **Google Business Profile** — business.google.com — the highest priority by far. Your GBP feeds directly into Google Search and Google Maps results. No other platform comes close in importance.
2. **Yelp** — biz.yelp.com — still one of the most heavily trafficked restaurant discovery platforms. Many guests check Yelp specifically before trying a new restaurant. A complete, photo-rich Yelp profile with recent reviews drives real covers.
3. **TripAdvisor** — tripadvisor.com/owners — critical if you serve tourists or if your neighborhood draws visitors. TripAdvisor listings also feed into other hotel and travel booking platforms.
4. **Facebook Business Page** — a complete Facebook page with accurate hours, address, menu, and recent posts counts as a citation and also appears in Facebook and Instagram search results. Many guests check your Facebook page when they cannot reach your website.
5. **OpenTable or Zomato (depending on your market)** — OpenTable is dominant in the U.S. for online reservations and feeds Google's "Reserve a table" button. Zomato is more relevant in international markets and some U.S. cities. If you take reservations and are not on one of these platforms, you are missing a significant discovery channel.

**How to audit existing listings:** Use the free scan at **Moz Local** (moz.com/local) or the 14-day trial at **BrightLocal** (brightlocal.com). Enter your restaurant name and address, and the tool will surface every online mention of your business and flag inconsistencies. Work through the flagged listings platform by platform, correcting the NAP to match your chosen canonical format. This audit takes 2-3 hours the first time but typically takes only 30 minutes per quarter to maintain.

## SECTION 3 — EMAIL LIST BUILDING

### How to Collect Emails at the Table

Your email list is the only marketing channel you own outright. Instagram can change its algorithm. Google can update how it ranks profiles. But your email list — that is yours. Every address in it represents someone who has already visited you and said, voluntarily, "I want to hear from you again." Building that list is one of the most valuable long-term investments in your restaurant's marketing.

**Method 1 — Check presenter insert:** Design a small card (business card size or 3x5 inch) that sits inside every check presenter. The card should offer something tangible in exchange for signing up: "Join our Insider List and get 15% off your next visit." Include a QR code linking to your email signup form and a short URL as a backup. The offer should be specific and immediately valuable — a discount on the next visit, a free appetizer, or a free dessert on their birthday. This method works because the guest is already in a positive mindset at the end of a good meal.

**Method 2 — QR code on the table or menu:** Place a QR code on your table tent card, on the back cover of your menu, or on a small tabletop sign. The QR code links to a simple signup landing page — either a Mailchimp embedded form or a Google Form with fields for first name, email, and birthday (month and day only). The landing page should take 15 seconds to complete and immediately confirm the signup with a thank-you message and a reminder of the reward they will receive.

**Method 3 — Verbal ask by the server:** Train every server to make the ask at the end of a positive table interaction. The script: "Would you like to join our insider list? We send exclusive deals and sneak peeks a couple of times a month — no spam, just good stuff. I can hand you a card with a QR code, or just write your email on the back of the receipt and I'll add you manually." Most guests who had a genuinely good experience are receptive. Train servers to read the table first — ask when the energy is warm and the guest seems satisfied, not when they are in a hurry.

**Method 4 — WiFi login capture:** If you offer guest Wi-Fi, use a router that supports a captive portal — a landing page that appears before granting Wi-Fi access. Services like **Beambox**, **Repeat Returns**, or **IgnitePOST** integrate with common restaurant Wi-Fi setups and capture the guest's email address (and optionally name and birthday) before they connect. The guest sees it as a fair exchange for free Wi-Fi. You see it as passive, consistent list growth that requires no staff involvement. Unifi routers with captive portal firmware are a solid mid-tier hardware option. Several paid restaurant marketing Wi-Fi platforms exist in the $50-$150/month range and handle the opt-in compliance for you.

### Loyalty Program Basics Without an App

You do not need a custom app, a paid loyalty platform, or a complicated points system to run an effective restaurant loyalty program. The most consistently redeemed loyalty programs at independent restaurants are the simplest ones.

**Setting it up in Mailchimp (free up to 500 contacts):** Create a new audience in Mailchimp specifically for loyalty members. Add custom fields: "Birthday Month," "Birthday Day," "Visit Count" (update manually or ask guests to report when they complete a punch card). Create a loyalty welcome email that fires automatically when someone joins this audience. The welcome email delivers the signup incentive immediately.

**What to offer as the loyalty reward:** Free appetizer after 5 visits or a free dessert after 10 visits are the most commonly redeemed restaurant loyalty rewards. Free dessert is the highest-redemption-rate reward and the lowest food cost — it costs you a few dollars but feels like genuine generosity. Free entree after 10 visits drives the deepest loyalty but requires more visits to redeem. Choose based on your average visit frequency and what feels meaningful to your specific guests.

**How to track visits with a stamp card:** Issue a physical stamp card to every guest who signs up for the loyalty program. The card has 10 boxes. Each visit gets a hand stamp or a hole punch. When a guest completes the card, they photograph it and email or text it to you, or bring it in and exchange it for the reward. This system works on the honor principle — most guests who have visited 10 times are genuine regulars who are not gaming you. The cost of the occasional abuse is far lower than the value of the loyalty behavior you are encouraging.

**Why simplicity beats complexity for independent restaurants:** Every layer of complexity you add to your loyalty program — point conversions, tier levels, expiration rules, app downloads required — reduces participation. Independent restaurant guests do not want to download another app. They want to feel recognized and appreciated. A simple stamp card communicates "we value your business" in a tangible, tactile way that an app notification cannot.

**What the loyalty welcome email should say:** Thank them immediately. Deliver the promised incentive immediately and clearly ("Here's your 15% off code: LOYAL15 — just show this email to your server on your next visit"). Explain the program in one sentence ("Every 10 visits, you earn a complimentary dessert — on us"). Tell them what to expect from future emails ("You'll hear from us about weekly specials, upcoming events, and member-only offers"). Close personally, from the owner or chef by name.

### What to Send and How Often

**Frequency:** Send a maximum of once per week. For most independent restaurants, biweekly (every two weeks) is the sweet spot — frequent enough to stay top of mind, infrequent enough that guests do not start ignoring you. If you have a weekly special program or live music every week, once a week is appropriate. If your content is more curated, biweekly or monthly is fine.

**What restaurant emails should contain:** Weekly specials (what is on the menu this week, written with appetite-inducing specificity — not "pasta special" but "hand-rolled pappardelle with slow-braised lamb shoulder, caramelized onion, and aged pecorino"), upcoming events (wine dinner, live music, holiday menu), behind-the-scenes moments (the morning the produce order arrives, the chef testing a new dish, the prep cook who has been with you for eight years), and member-only perks (a discount or early access offer available only to email subscribers).

**Five strong subject line examples:**
1. "The dish our chef almost took off the menu (and why guests won't let him)"
2. "This Friday only: half-price oysters from 5-7pm — for our list only"
3. "We tried 11 versions of this pasta. Here's the one that made it to the menu."
4. "Your weekend plans just got better (new summer menu inside)"
5. "A behind-the-scenes look at where our vegetables come from"

**The 80/20 rule for restaurant email content:** Eighty percent of your emails should deliver value — stories, restaurant behind-the-scenes content, dish origin stories, team spotlights, supplier spotlights, neighborhood news. Twenty percent can be promotional — a specific offer, a discount, a limited-time event with a price. Readers who feel like your emails are always selling them something will unsubscribe. Readers who feel like your emails are interesting and occasionally include a bonus offer will stay forever.

**Best send days for restaurant emails:** Tuesday and Wednesday consistently produce higher open rates for restaurant emails than Monday (too busy catching up), Friday (guests have already made plans), or weekend (inbox is deprioritized). Send between 10am and 2pm on Tuesday or Wednesday. Set this as your standard send window and stick to it — predictability helps readers develop the habit of opening your emails.

## SECTION 4 — INFLUENCER & FOOD BLOGGER OUTREACH

### How to Find Local Food Bloggers and Creators

The right local food creator can send you more reservation requests in 72 hours than three months of your own social media content. The key word is "local" — a creator with 8,000 highly engaged followers in your neighborhood is worth more to you than a creator with 500,000 followers distributed across the country. Here is how to find them.

**Search Instagram using your city's location tag:** Open Instagram, tap the search icon, and search your city name. Switch to the "Places" tab to see location-tagged posts. Browse recent posts and look for food content. When you find a photo of a restaurant or dish that is clearly professionally shot or thoughtfully composed, click the account. Look at their full feed — are they consistently posting food content? Are they local? Do their posts consistently show local restaurants and cafes, or are they scattered across multiple cities?

**Search Instagram hashtags:** Search #[yourcity]eats, #[yourcity]food, #[yourcity]foodie, #[yourcity]restaurants, #[yourneighborhood]eats. Browse the "Recent" tab, not "Top," to find active local creators. The "Top" tab shows the highest-engagement posts, which are often from larger accounts. The "Recent" tab shows you who is actively posting, including micro-creators with smaller but highly engaged local audiences.

**Google search for food bloggers:** Search "[your city] food blog 2024," "[your city] food Instagram," "[your city] restaurant review blog," and "best food accounts [your city]." These searches often surface curated lists, magazine articles, and "best local foodies to follow" roundups that hand you a pre-vetted list. Save every name you find to a spreadsheet.

**Search TikTok:** Search your city name plus "food" on TikTok and filter results by "Accounts." Many younger food creators are building their primary audience on TikTok now and have strong local followings. Filter by "Location" in the search settings if the option is available. Look for creators who consistently geo-tag their videos at local restaurants.

**Join local Facebook foodie groups:** Search "[Your City] Foodies," "[Your City] Food and Drink," "[Your City] Restaurant Recommendations," and "[Your Neighborhood] Community." Join the groups and observe for a week before posting. The most active, most-liked contributors in these groups are often informal local food influencers — they may not have large Instagram followings, but their recommendations carry enormous weight in the community.

**What to look for before reaching out:** Genuine engagement (likes and comments from real local people — check that comments are authentic, not generic), posting frequency (at least 2-3 food posts per week shows this is a real content focus), local audience (look at the comment section for local names, places, and references), restaurant price point alignment (their existing restaurant features should be similar to yours in price point and style — a $200-per-person tasting menu and a $40-per-head neighborhood Italian restaurant attract different audiences), and content authenticity (photos that show the actual dining experience, honest captions, real reactions — not obviously staged "partnership content" energy on every post).

### What to Offer: Comp Meal vs. Paid Partnership

**When a comp meal is appropriate:** A complimentary meal for two (or occasionally four, if the creator tends to shoot group shots) is the right offer when you are building a new relationship with a creator whose audience you have not yet measured, when the creator has under 10,000 followers and primarily works on an exchange basis, when you want to test the relationship before committing a paid budget, or when you are a newer restaurant still building your brand and the cost of a full meal represents a manageable marketing expense. The comp meal has no contractual obligation attached to it — it preserves the authenticity of any content the creator chooses to make, and it keeps the relationship warm and flexible. Most honest food creators appreciate the no-strings approach and are more likely to post enthusiastically when they do not feel like they owe you something.

**When a paid partnership makes sense:** A paid partnership is appropriate when the creator has an established, engaged local audience (typically 10,000+ followers with meaningful engagement — comments, saves, shares from local accounts), when you need guaranteed deliverables on a specific timeline (a new menu launch, a grand reopening, a seasonal event you want promoted during a specific week), when the creator explicitly works on a paid basis only, or when you want contractual usage rights to the content they create (the right to repurpose their Reels or photos in your own paid social ads). Paid partnerships require a written agreement.

**How to structure a paid partnership for a small restaurant:** Define deliverables clearly in writing before money changes hands. A reasonable minimum for a local micro-influencer paid deal: 1 Instagram Reel plus 3 Instagram Stories, to be posted within [specific date range]. Usage rights should be specified — request a 60-90 day window to use their content in your own paid social ads and email. Typical rates for local food creators as of 2025: $150-$500 for micro-influencers (5K-25K followers), $500-$2,000 for mid-tier creators (25K-100K followers). These are estimates — rates vary significantly by market, engagement rate, and deliverables. Always confirm rates in a brief email exchange before inviting them.

**What to expect from organic vs. paid:** Organic (comp meal, no obligation) content tends to be more authentic, more enthusiastic, and more believable to audiences — when it happens. You cannot count on it or control it. Paid content gives you scheduled deliverables, usage rights, and the ability to brief on specific messaging points (mentioning a particular dish, using your hashtag, tagging your location). Both have value. For independent restaurants, building a roster of organic creator relationships through comp meals first, then converting your 2-3 strongest partnerships to paid arrangements for major campaigns, is the most cost-effective approach.

### How to Use User-Generated Content (UGC)

UGC — any photo, video, or post created by a guest about your restaurant — is your most powerful and cheapest form of social proof. A guest's authentic photo of your food, shared to their own audience, reaches people who have never heard of you through a voice they already trust. Here is how to build a system around it.

**Asking permission to repost:** Always ask, every time. Send a DM directly to the guest: "We love this photo! Would you mind if we shared it on our page? We'll credit you fully with a tag." The answer is almost always yes. Do not repost without asking — it is both legally risky and a breach of trust. For accounts with fewer than 1,000 followers, replying to their post publicly ("This is stunning! Mind if we share? We'll tag you!") is fine. For larger accounts, a private DM is more professional.

**How to credit properly:** Tag their handle in the photo itself (not just in the caption) AND mention them in the caption text ("Photo by the talented @[guesthandle] — we love seeing this dish through your eyes"). Double crediting — both in the tag and the caption — ensures they get notified and makes the credit visible to anyone reading the caption.

**Building a UGC library:** Create a dedicated Google Drive folder called "UGC Archive." Every time you see a tagged post or story about your restaurant, screenshot it and save it to the folder, organized by month. After 3-4 months, you will have a library of authentic guest photos you can repurpose in email newsletters, on your website gallery, in paid social ads (with permission), and in your GBP profile. This archive becomes a marketing asset with genuine monetary value.

**How to encourage guests to tag you:** Print your Instagram handle on the inside cover of your menu. Add it to your to-go bag sticker or stamp. Put it on your table tent cards with a specific prompt ("Snap it. Tag us. @[handle]. We repost our favorites."). Add it to the back of your business cards. A small sign near the restrooms ("You're already on your phone — follow us @[handle]") captures guests at a natural phone-check moment. Add the handle to your receipt footer and your WiFi password card.

**Monthly guest photo feature:** Announce a "guest photo of the month" program — each month you repost the best guest photo and give the photographer a small reward (a free appetizer on their next visit, a complimentary glass of wine, a $15 credit). Announce the winner publicly on your social media. This incentive encourages tagging, rewards loyal guests, and creates positive buzz every time you announce a winner.

### Dos and Don'ts

**Do:**
1. Do research every creator before reaching out — reference something specific they have posted to show you are a real human who actually follows their work, not a mass email blaster.
2. Do give creators complete freedom during their visit — do not hover, do not over-explain the menu, do not ask if they are getting "good content." Let the experience speak for itself.
3. Do follow up once, and only once, if you do not hear back within 7-10 days. A single brief follow-up is professional. A third email is not.
4. Do give every creator a direct contact (your personal cell or a direct email) and make booking their visit seamless — offer multiple dates, accommodate any dietary needs immediately, and confirm all details in writing.
5. Do thank every creator after their visit regardless of whether they post. A handwritten note or a thoughtful email goes a long way toward building a long-term relationship.

**Don't:**
1. Don't demand a specific post, a specific number of photos, or a positive review from a creator you have not paid — that is not how organic relationships work and will damage the relationship immediately.
2. Don't make the creator feel like a content machine during their meal — bring them a course description if they ask, but otherwise treat them exactly as you would any valued guest.
3. Don't request edits to published content unless something is factually incorrect (a wrong dish name, a wrong price). Asking someone to change their genuine opinion or soften a critique is not acceptable.
4. Don't use any creator's content without explicit permission, even if it is flattering. Always ask. Always credit. Always honor the agreement.
5. Don't invite 8 creators to dine on the same night without disclosing this to them — an unannounced "influencer table" event feels inauthentic to both the creators and their audiences, and it undermines the genuine experience you are trying to create.

## SECTION 5 — NEIGHBORHOOD BUSINESS PARTNERSHIPS

### Cross-Promotion Ideas with Complementary Businesses

The businesses around you are your neighbors, and they share your customers. Someone who buys wine from the shop on your block is exactly the kind of person who might dine at your restaurant. Someone who buys flowers for a special occasion is already in a celebratory mindset. Building relationships with complementary businesses creates a web of referrals that compounds over time — every partnership you establish adds a new stream of introductions to people who are predisposed to spend at a place like yours.

**Wine shop partnership:** Propose a mutual referral arrangement where a bottle purchase at the wine shop includes a dinner recommendation card from your restaurant ("This bottle pairs beautifully with our braised short rib — reserve your table at [Restaurant Name] and mention this card for a complimentary bread course"). In exchange, you feature their wines on your by-the-glass or bottle menu with their branding ("Sourced exclusively from [Wine Shop Name], two blocks away"). Co-host a quarterly wine and food pairing event where they educate on the wine and you provide paired small plates — sell tickets, split the revenue, promote to both email lists.

**Florist partnership:** Propose using their seasonal arrangements as your centerpieces, featuring their branding on a small card at each table and tagging them in every weekly "table setting" photo you post. In exchange, they include your menu card in wedding and event consultations ("Looking for a rehearsal dinner or engagement party venue? Our friends at [Restaurant Name] are the best in the neighborhood."). For Mother's Day, Valentine's Day, and other floral holidays, bundle a "dinner and flowers" package: reserve through you, get a small complimentary floral arrangement to take home.

**Event venue partnership:** Identify any event venue, private club, or meeting facility within a 10-minute drive. Offer to be their preferred "after-event" dining recommendation for groups — corporate dinners, wedding party meals, graduation celebrations. In exchange, they become your preferred referral for any guest who needs a private venue for a party larger than your private dining capacity. Offer their event clients a 10% group discount as an incentive to book with you. Have a stack of your menus at their front desk.

**Coffee roaster partnership:** Propose an exclusive arrangement where you serve their beans and co-brand the coffee section of your menu ("Our espresso and pour-over coffee is sourced exclusively from [Roaster Name], roasted locally in [neighborhood]"). This gives the roaster visibility to your entire dining audience. In exchange, they place your restaurant menu and a "dinner recommendation" card at their counter, and they mention you in their coffee bag inserts if they do direct-to-consumer sales. Co-host a "coffee and brunch" event or a late-night espresso + dessert pairing event together.

**Local brewery partnership:** Approach a nearby craft brewery about a seasonal beer dinner collaboration — twice a year, you design a tasting menu specifically paired with their current draft lineup. They send the event to their email list and post about it; you do the same. Tickets sell out because both audiences trust both brands. Year-round, feature their seasonal tap on your draft list and tag them whenever you post a photo of it. Ask them to include your restaurant on their "where to eat nearby" recommendation board — most taprooms have one, and they get asked for dinner recommendations constantly.

### How to Pitch a Local Partnership

**The in-person walk-in:** This is the most effective approach for businesses within walking distance. Walk in during a mid-morning quiet period (avoid lunch rush), ask for the owner or manager by name if you know it, and introduce yourself simply: "Hi, I'm [Name] — I'm the owner of [Restaurant Name], just around the corner on [street]. I've been a fan of what you do here and I wanted to introduce myself. I have a quick idea about a simple cross-promotion between our two businesses if you have two minutes." Keep it brief, warm, and specific. Offer one concrete idea on the spot so the conversation has something tangible to react to: "I'd love to display your cards at our host stand if you'd be open to doing the same for us — zero cost, just community." Then suggest coffee to explore further: "Would you have time for a 20-minute coffee in the next week or two? I'm happy to come to you."

**The email version (for businesses you cannot visit in person):** Keep it short — five sentences maximum. Subject: "A quick partnership idea between [Their Business] and [Your Restaurant]." Body: "Hi [Name], I'm [Name], owner of [Restaurant Name] on [street], [X blocks] from you. I think our customers overlap significantly and I have a simple cross-promotion idea that could benefit both of us — [one-sentence description of the specific idea]. Would you be open to a 20-minute coffee to explore? I'm happy to come to you and I can work around your schedule." That is it. Short, specific, non-demanding, and it gives them everything they need to say yes or no without wading through a pitch deck.

### What to Offer in a Partnership

**Mutual social media mentions:** Each business posts about the other once per month. The post should feel genuine — a photo of their product, a personal recommendation, a story about why you love what they do. Both accounts tag each other. Each partner's audience sees a trusted local business vouching for another trusted local business. The combined audience reach is free.

**Physical flyer or menu card exchange:** Each business keeps a small stack of the other's materials (menu card, business card, flyer, postcard) at their counter or register. Customers who come in for one thing get introduced to the other. Low effort, zero cost, ongoing passive referral.

**Joint email newsletter mention:** Once per quarter, each business mentions the other in their email newsletter. A one-paragraph feature ("We are proud to partner with [Business Name], just down the street. If you haven't visited, here's why you should...") with a link. Both businesses sign off on the mention before it sends. The cost is a paragraph of writing; the benefit is an introduction to an entire audience that already trusts the sender.

**Cross-discount arrangement:** Show a receipt from one business for 10% off (or a complimentary welcome item) at the other. This creates a measurable referral loop and gives both businesses a concrete way to track whether the partnership is working. It also creates a reason for the business's staff to actively recommend you: "Did you know you can show your receipt here for a discount at [Restaurant Name]?"

**Co-hosted event:** The highest-investment, highest-reward partnership format. A wine and food pairing event, a coffee and brunch morning, a "date night package" promoted together. Each partner promotes to their own list and social following. Tickets are sold through one platform (Eventbrite, Tock, or just a Venmo/direct payment system). Proceeds are split or applied proportionally based on each partner's contribution. The attendees experience both brands at their best, together.

**Date night package:** Bundle a romantic evening across three local businesses — dinner at your restaurant, flowers from the florist, and wine from the shop. Market it together as "The [Neighborhood] Date Night Package." Guests book the experience as a single unit. All three businesses promote it. The package creates a memorable, shareable experience that spreads organically. Charge a premium for the bundle; split the revenue proportionally.

## SECTION 6 — OFFLINE MARKETING THAT STILL WORKS

### Table Tent Cards

A table tent is a folded card that stands upright on each table in your dining room. While guests are sitting, finishing a drink, or waiting for their next course, they are reading everything in front of them. Table tent cards are free impressions on a captive audience that already likes you.

**What to put on your table tent card:** Your Instagram handle prominently at the top in a large, readable font — this is the single most important piece of information on the card. A QR code that links directly to either your Instagram profile or your email list signup (choose one per card — do not try to put both). A specific, compelling prompt: "Follow us @[handle] — we post daily specials every morning" gives guests a concrete reason to follow beyond obligation. A secondary QR code (on the back, or lower on the front) linking to your Google review page with the prompt "Loved your meal? Leave us a quick review — it means the world to a small restaurant like ours."

**Design your table tent in Canva:** Canva (canva.com) has free table tent templates. Use your brand colors, a single strong food photo, and keep the layout simple. Resist the urge to put too much on the card — a cluttered tent card gets ignored. Choose one primary call to action and one secondary call to action.

**Updating frequency:** Update your table tent cards seasonally — at minimum every 3 months, or whenever you have a major promotion or event worth announcing. Tent cards that are torn, stained, or out of date communicate carelessness. Inspect every card every week and replace any that are showing wear. A clean, current table tent card reinforces your standards; a dirty one undermines them.

**Ordering:** Print double-sided in cardstock (minimum 100 lb cover weight so they stand up and hold their shape) at Vistaprint, Canva Print, or a local print shop. Order 50-100 at a time. A run of 100 high-quality 4x6 inch folded table tents costs $40-$80 from most online printers.

### Birthday Club

The birthday email is the highest-converting restaurant marketing message you will ever send. It arrives at exactly the right moment — when someone is already thinking about celebration, already looking for a reason to go out, and already feeling sentimental. The redemption rates for well-executed birthday club offers are consistently higher than any other email campaign type.

**Collecting birthdays:** Add a "Birthday" field to your email signup form — just month and day, never the year. Many people are uncomfortable sharing their birth year with a business; month and day is all you need to trigger the automation. Frame it positively: "Birthday (just month + day — we'll send you something special!)." Also collect birthdays on your paper loyalty card enrollment and verbally at the end of a positive table interaction: "We have a birthday club where we send you a special treat every year around your birthday — want me to grab you a sign-up card?"

**What to send:** Send an email 3 days before their birthday. Three days gives them enough time to make a reservation and plan their evening, but it is close enough to feel personally relevant rather than a form letter sent three weeks early. The email should have a warm, personal subject line ("A little birthday surprise for you, [First Name]") and should include a specific, clearly stated offer with no confusing conditions.

**What to offer:** A free dessert has the highest redemption rate and the lowest food cost of any birthday offer. It feels meaningful ("they remembered my birthday and sent me something real") without costing you more than a few dollars in food cost. Free appetizer is a close second. A 15% discount on the full check is popular but has a lower redemption rate because it requires the guest to do math and the dollar value feels less tangible than a specific free item. Choose free dessert or free appetizer as your default.

**How to set up birthday automation in Mailchimp:** In Mailchimp, go to Automations and create a new Customer Journey. Set the trigger as "Birthday" (Mailchimp has a native birthday trigger). Set the action as "Send email" and set the timing as "3 days before birthday." Mailchimp will send the email automatically each year to every subscriber who has provided their birthday, with no ongoing effort from you. This automation, once built, will run for years. Add a secondary "Day of" email as a second step in the sequence if you want a reminder for guests who have not yet redeemed.

**What the birthday email should say:** "Happy almost-birthday, [First Name]. We want to celebrate with you. Come in within the next week and mention your birthday to your server — we have a complimentary [dessert/appetizer] waiting for you, on the house, no questions asked. It's our way of saying thank you for another year of dining with us. We hope to see you soon."

### Window Signage for Social Media

Your restaurant's window faces your street. Every person who walks past is a potential follower, a potential guest, a potential regular. Use that surface.

**What to put in your window:** A bold "Follow us on Instagram: @[handle]" displayed in your brand's font, large enough to read from the sidewalk. The handle should be a minimum of 3-4 inches tall on a printed sign, or at least the same size as your business name on your signage. Include the Instagram logo icon to make it immediately recognizable. Below or beside the handle, include a QR code linked directly to your Instagram profile — this lets passersby scan and follow immediately without typing anything. Add a specific reason to follow: "Daily specials posted every morning" or "See what's coming off the grill tonight" gives people a content-based reason to follow, not just a courtesy ask.

**Size and placement:** Minimum 8.5 x 11 inches, but ideally 18 x 24 inches for visibility from across the street. Place it at eye level — between 4 and 6 feet from the ground — on a window near the entrance or on your most visible street-facing window. If you have a fence or sandwich board near the sidewalk, add the handle there too.

**Where to print affordably:** Vistaprint and Canva Print both offer printed window vinyl or foam board prints for $25-$60. Your local print shop can produce window static cling decals for $30-$80. The decal is the most professional option — it looks designed rather than taped-on. Alternatively, a high-quality foam board print on an easel just inside the window achieves similar visibility at lower cost.

### Loyalty Punch Cards

The physical punch card is not obsolete. For independent restaurants whose guests skew toward 35 and older, the tangible card outperforms digital loyalty apps in enrollment and redemption. Guests carry it in their wallet. They see it when they reach for cash. It is a tiny physical reminder of your restaurant sitting in their pocket.

**The 10-visit card system:** Design a card with 10 boxes. Each box gets a hand stamp or hole punch at the end of every visit. The 10th completed visit earns a free [signature item] — something desirable but not your highest food-cost item. A free appetizer, free dessert, or house cocktail is the sweet spot: guests feel like they have earned something genuinely good, and you keep your food cost manageable.

**Handling the honor system:** Most of your regulars — the people who will realistically complete 10 visits — are honest. The cost of the occasional guest who fills in their own boxes is trivially small compared to the loyalty value generated by the program overall. Trust your regulars; they are not gaming you.

**The digital alternative for guests who prefer it:** Tell any guest who prefers not to carry a paper card: "Just photograph your completed card and text it to [your number] — we'll add the reward to your next visit." This removes the "I always forget my card" objection without requiring you to build any technology infrastructure.

**What the reward should be:** It should feel like a genuine gift, not a grudging discount. A free appetizer says "we appreciate you" more convincingly than "10% off your 11th visit." Choose something from your menu that photographs well, tastes impressive, and makes the guest feel celebrated — then make sure every staff member is trained to deliver it with genuine warmth: "You earned this — enjoy it, truly."

**Where to print:** Design the card in Canva (free templates available) and print at Vistaprint or your local print shop. Roughly $20-$40 for 100 cards on heavy cardstock. Reorder whenever your stock drops below 20.

### "Tag Us" Prompts

Turning every guest into a potential content creator requires only one thing: making the ask easy and specific. Vague "share us on social" messaging gets ignored. Specific, placed prompts in the right moments work.

**Places to add your "tag us" prompt:**

- **Inside the menu cover or on the back page:** "Snap a photo and tag us @[YourHandle] — we repost our favorites!" Include your handle in the same font size as the menu item names so it reads as part of the menu, not as an afterthought.
- **On the check presenter:** Print your handle directly on the check presenter leather or on a small card tucked inside. When guests are reviewing the bill — a natural phone moment — your handle is right there.
- **On a small card in to-go bags:** Every to-go order bag should include a card that says "Tag us when you dig in @[handle]" alongside your loyalty signup QR code. People photograph delivery food constantly.
- **On food containers:** A circular sticker on your takeout container lid with your handle and a small camera icon. "Photo it. Tag us. @[handle]." Cost: $15-$30 for a custom sticker run at Sticker Mule.
- **A small sign near the restrooms:** Guests check their phones near restrooms. A small framed print ("You're probably on your phone right now — might as well follow us. @[handle]") is lighthearted, memorable, and effective.
- **The WiFi password card:** If you give guests a card with the WiFi password, put your Instagram handle directly below the password. They will be on their phones the moment they connect.
- **The receipt footer:** Customize your POS receipt footer (available in Square, Toast, and Clover) to include: "Tag us @[handle] and we'll share your photo!" with your review link below it.

**Exact wording that works:** "Snap it. Tag us. @[YourHandle]" is the most direct and highest-converting phrase. Alternatively: "Share your meal and tag @[handle] — we repost our favorites!" The key elements are: a specific action verb, your exact handle, and a reason to do it (the repost promise).

## SECTION 7 — 90-DAY LOCAL MARKETING PLAN

### Month 1 — Foundation

The goal of Month 1 is infrastructure. By the end of this month, you will have a complete Google Business Profile, a functioning email signup system, your first offline materials in place, and your team trained on every guest-facing touchpoint. You are not trying to go viral in Month 1. You are building the systems that make growth possible and sustainable.

**Week 1 — Google Business Profile Audit and Completion**

Work through every item in the Section 1 Complete Setup Checklist this week. Treat it as a one-time intensive project. Set aside 3-4 hours across two or three sessions. By Friday of Week 1: your GBP should be fully verified, every field completed, 25+ photos uploaded across all categories, the Q&A section pre-seeded with at least 5 questions and answers, the messaging feature enabled with an auto-reply written, and your first GBP post published. Also this week: identify your 3 core content pillars for the month — the recurring themes your posts will orbit around. Examples: "the food and the craft behind it," "our team and the culture of the kitchen," "neighborhood and community." Block your posting days in your calendar for the full month (3 days per week minimum — pick the same days each week).

**Week 2 — Email Signup System Setup and Google Review Link**

Set up a free Mailchimp account. Create an audience. Build a signup form with first name, email, and birthday (month and day). Generate the form's unique link and create a QR code pointing to it. Design your table tent card in Canva using your brand colors and the QR code — order a print run this week (Vistaprint or Canva Print) so cards arrive by Week 4. Generate your Google review link (follow the steps in Section 1) and shorten it with bit.ly. Create a QR code for the review link separately. Share both links with every staff member via group text: "Here is our Google review link. When a guest has a great experience, you can offer to text it to them. Here's the script: 'If you'd like to leave us a review, it takes about 30 seconds — want me to text you the link?'" Also this week: add your Instagram handle to your menu if it is not already there.

**Week 3 — Email List Building Starts and Research Phase**

Your Mailchimp form is live this week. Start sharing the signup link with your first 10 regulars personally — text it directly with a short note: "We're starting an insider email list with exclusive offers — I'd love to add you if you're interested." This personal ask from the owner converts at a much higher rate than a generic QR code. Begin building your list of 5-10 local businesses for potential future partnerships — write down their names, what they do, and one specific cross-promotion idea for each. This week, research local food hashtags in your market: search your neighborhood and city on Instagram and compile the top 30 relevant hashtags (mix of high-volume and niche). Save these to a notes document — you will use them in every post. Begin setting up your Google Business posting schedule in your calendar.

**Week 4 — Materials Arrive, Staff Training, and First Newsletter**

Your table tent cards arrive this week — put one on every table immediately. Order loyalty punch cards if you have not already. Design and order your Instagram handle window vinyl (Vistaprint or local print shop) if you plan to install one. This is your staff training week: hold a 15-minute pre-shift meeting to brief all front-of-house staff on the full guest-facing marketing system: how to offer the loyalty card, how to use the email signup QR code, how to prompt the "tag us" request when delivering photogenic dishes, and how to offer the Google review link at the end of a positive interaction. Make sure every staff member has the review link saved on their phone. By the end of Week 4: publish your first email newsletter to whatever list you have built so far, even if it is only 15 people. Note your current review count on Google, Yelp, and TripAdvisor as your baseline for measuring Month 2 and Month 3 progress.

### Month 2 — Community

The goal of Month 2 is to extend your reach into the community that surrounds you — both the online community of local food creators and the physical community of neighboring businesses. The infrastructure you built in Month 1 now becomes the foundation you build relationships on top of.

**Week 5 — Creator Research and First Outreach Batch**

Spend 2-3 hours this week using the methods in Section 4 to identify 10-15 local food content creators. Build a simple spreadsheet with columns for: creator name, handle, follower count, estimated engagement rate, email or DM contact, outreach status, and notes. Write personalized outreach emails to your top 5-8 creators using the template in Section 4 — personalize every single one by referencing something specific they have posted. Send the first batch of 5 outreach emails by Wednesday. Follow each creator on Instagram this week and engage genuinely with their content before and after sending — like posts, leave thoughtful comments, respond to stories. This human engagement makes your outreach email land differently than a cold pitch. Also this week: identify one local event or community moment in the coming month that you can tie content to (a neighborhood festival, a sports team's playoff run, a local charity event).

**Week 6 — First Business Partnership Conversation**

Pick the single most natural local business partner from the list you built in Week 3 of Month 1 and walk in this week to introduce yourself. Use the in-person pitch script from Section 5. Keep it brief, warm, and specific. The goal of this conversation is not to close a deal — it is to open a relationship. Suggest coffee to discuss further. Plan one in-house event or special offer for Week 8 of this month — decide on the format, date, and the other business (if any) you want to co-host with. Set up the birthday club automation in Mailchimp this week: create the "Birthday" campaign, write the birthday email, and configure the 3-day-before send trigger. Once built, this automation runs forever.

**Week 7 — UGC, Staff Review Training, and Content Batch**

Review your tagged posts from the past month. Reach out via DM to anyone who has posted a photo of your food and request permission to repost. Repost your first piece of approved UGC this week, crediting the guest fully in both the tag and the caption. Hold a focused staff training session specifically on Google review requests — role-play the conversation with your team until it feels natural. The script: "If you had a great time tonight, a Google review means the world to us as a small restaurant. I can text you the direct link if you'd like — it takes about 30 seconds." Practice until it sounds human, not scripted. Spend 90 minutes this week batching content: photograph 10-15 dishes, write 6-8 caption drafts, and schedule posts for the next two weeks in Meta Business Suite (free) so you are not posting day-of.

**Week 8 — Event or Special, Second Newsletter, Review Count Check**

Host the in-house event or run the special you planned in Week 6. Photograph it thoroughly. Send your second email newsletter to your list this week — make this one a behind-the-scenes story or a spotlight on your most beloved dish. Check your review count on Google and Yelp against the baseline you recorded at the end of Month 1. Any growth, however small, is worth celebrating with your team. Share the number at a pre-shift meeting: "We had 47 Google reviews at the start of last month. We now have 54. That is 7 more people who took time to publicly recommend us. Thank you." Visible progress motivates the behaviors that create it.

### Month 3 — Growth

The goal of Month 3 is to build on everything you have established, introduce modest paid amplification, and create the habits and systems that will sustain your marketing indefinitely beyond this 90-day plan.

**Week 9-10 — Paid Social Awareness Campaign**

You have now spent two months building organic content and a real local audience. You have your best-performing posts from the past 60 days to work with. This is the right moment to introduce a small paid social campaign. Go to Meta Business Suite and create a new campaign with the objective set to "Reach" or "Awareness" — not "Conversions" and not "Lead generation." Your goal at this stage is simply to get your restaurant in front of people within a 3-5 mile radius who do not yet know you. Set a budget of $5-$10 per day. Select your target audience: location (3-5 mile radius around your restaurant), age (25-55, or adjust based on your actual customer demographic), interests (dining out, food, local restaurants, specific cuisine types). Boost your single best-performing organic post from the past 60 days — choose the post with the most engagement (likes + comments + saves) as your ad creative. Do not create a new ad from scratch; use what your audience has already validated. Run the campaign for 14 days. Monitor reach and impressions daily. If click-through rate is below 1% at day 7, swap the creative for a different post. Do not touch the audience targeting or budget mid-run — let one variable change at a time.

**Week 11 — Loyalty Program Launch and Email Optimization**

If you have been running a punch card program informally, formalize it this week: announce it in your email newsletter ("We have officially launched our loyalty program — here is how it works"), brief staff again to offer cards to every table, and ensure cards are stocked at the host stand, bar, and in every check presenter. Confirm that your birthday club automation is active and has sent at least a few test emails (Mailchimp allows you to preview and test). Review your email open rates from the past two months. Your target open rate is 25% or higher — this is healthy for an independent restaurant email list. Subject lines that contain a specific dish name, a number, or a first-person curiosity hook ("The dish our chef almost took off the menu") consistently outperform generic subject lines ("This week's newsletter"). Apply those learnings to every future send.

**Week 12 — Quarterly Review and Quarter 2 Planning**

Celebrate any review milestone with your team publicly. If you set a baseline of 47 Google reviews 90 days ago and you now have 65, that is 18 people who took time to publicly recommend you — celebrate that number. Pull the metrics from your marketing tracking document: How many new email subscribers did you collect over 90 days? What is your current GBP review count compared to baseline? What was your average email open rate? Which Instagram posts generated the most engagement? Which piece of content drove the most direct action (reservations, walk-ins mentioning a post)? Use this data to write your Quarter 2 90-day plan. Identify the single marketing channel that produced the most measurable results this quarter and commit to doubling your investment in it in Q2 — whether that is more email frequency, more GBP posts, more creator outreach, or a slightly larger paid social budget. The restaurants that build lasting local presence are the ones that review what worked, do more of it, and keep showing up.

The businesses that become the default choice in their neighborhoods are not the ones that went viral once — they are the ones that showed up every week, built real relationships with real people, and made their community feel seen. Keep going.*
