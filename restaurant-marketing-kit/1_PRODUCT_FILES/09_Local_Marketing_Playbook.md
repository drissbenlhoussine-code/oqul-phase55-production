# Local Marketing & Community Growth Playbook

This playbook gives you a step-by-step system for growing your restaurant's presence in your local community — without a big budget or a marketing team. Every section is built around tactics that work for independent restaurants. Follow the 90-day plan at the end to put it all into motion.

---

## Section 1: Google Business Profile Optimization

Your Google Business Profile (GBP) is the single most important free marketing tool available to a local restaurant. When someone searches "restaurants near me" or "[your cuisine] in [your city]," your GBP is often the first thing they see — before your website, before your Instagram, before any review site. Getting this right is non-negotiable.

### Claiming and Verifying Your Profile

- [ ] Go to **business.google.com** and sign in with your Google account
- [ ] Search for your restaurant by name and address
- [ ] If a listing already exists, click "Claim this business" — do not create a duplicate
- [ ] If no listing exists, click "Add your business to Google" and follow the prompts
- [ ] Select the verification method (postcard by mail is most common; phone and email verification are offered to some businesses)
- [ ] If verifying by postcard: wait 5–14 days for the postcard to arrive, then enter the 5-digit code in your GBP dashboard
- [ ] Once verified, confirm your profile shows the green "Verified" checkmark in your dashboard

### Completing Your Profile to 100%

- [ ] **Business name**: Use your exact legal operating name — no keyword stuffing (e.g., do not write "Mario's Pizza - Best Pizza in Denver"; just "Mario's Pizza")
- [ ] **Address**: Enter the exact address that appears on your storefront signage and website — match it precisely
- [ ] **Phone number**: Use a local number with area code; avoid tracking numbers that differ from your main line
- [ ] **Website**: Link to your homepage or, if you have one, your direct reservations page
- [ ] **Hours**: Set accurate regular hours; add special hours for every holiday and closure
- [ ] **Primary category**: Choose the most specific option (e.g., "Italian Restaurant," not just "Restaurant")
- [ ] **Secondary categories**: Add up to 9 additional categories (e.g., "Pizza Restaurant," "Wine Bar," "Catering Food and Drink Supplier" if applicable)
- [ ] **Business description**: Write 250–750 words covering what makes you unique, your cuisine type, neighborhood name, signature dishes, and dining experience (see below for details)
- [ ] **Menu link**: Add your direct menu URL in the "Menu" field — use a link to your actual menu page, not just your homepage
- [ ] **Booking link**: Connect OpenTable, Resy, Tock, or your direct booking system in the "Reservations" field
- [ ] **Attributes**: Check every applicable attribute — Dine-in, Takeout, Delivery, Outdoor seating, Wheelchair accessible, Accepts credit cards, Free Wi-Fi, Kids' menu, etc.

### Writing Your Business Description

Your description is indexed by Google and influences how you appear in searches. Write it as if you're answering three questions: Who are you? What do you serve? Why should someone choose you?

**Keywords to naturally weave in:**
- Your cuisine type + neighborhood: "authentic Thai restaurant in Wicker Park"
- Occasion keywords: "perfect for date night," "family-friendly," "great for business lunches," "ideal for groups"
- Dish-specific keywords: name your 2–3 most famous dishes
- Experience keywords: "wood-fired," "farm-to-table," "locally sourced," "house-made pasta"
- Location modifiers: city, neighborhood, nearby landmark ("two blocks from the Art Museum")

**Example opening:**
"Rosario's is a family-owned Italian restaurant in Chicago's Andersonville neighborhood, serving hand-rolled pasta, wood-fired Neapolitan pizza, and seasonal dishes made with locally sourced ingredients since 2008. Whether you're planning a romantic dinner, celebrating a birthday, or feeding a hungry family, Rosario's offers a warm, welcoming dining room and some of the best house-made lasagna in Chicago's North Side."

- [ ] Write your description draft and paste it into the GBP "Description" field
- [ ] Confirm the description contains your city name, neighborhood name, and cuisine type
- [ ] Confirm the description mentions at least 2–3 signature dishes by name
- [ ] Confirm the description is between 250–750 characters (GBP limit)

### Photo Requirements

Photos are one of the top factors that influence whether someone clicks on your listing. Profiles with more than 100 photos receive significantly more direction requests and clicks than those with fewer than 10.

**Photo categories and targets:**

- [ ] **Exterior photos (minimum 5)**: Shot from the street showing your signage, entrance, and building facade. Include daytime and nighttime versions. Show the experience of arriving.
- [ ] **Interior photos (minimum 10)**: Full dining room, bar area, specific seating arrangements, ambient lighting. Capture the feel of the space — busy service energy AND quiet daytime warmth.
- [ ] **Food photos (minimum 20)**: Your top-selling dishes, seasonal specials, cocktails/drinks, desserts, and any signature items. Use natural light whenever possible.
- [ ] **Team photos (minimum 3)**: Chef(s), front-of-house staff, bartenders. These humanize your brand and build trust.
- [ ] **At-work/kitchen photos (minimum 5)**: Prep work, plating, the kitchen in motion, ingredients being sourced. These demonstrate craft and care.
- [ ] **Menu photos**: If you offer a physical menu, photograph it clearly so customers can browse before visiting
- [ ] Upload your logo as the "Logo" image
- [ ] Set the most compelling food photo as your "Cover" image
- [ ] Add short captions to each photo (Google allows captions — use them with relevant keywords)

**Photo specs:**
- Minimum 720 x 720 pixels; ideally 1080 x 1080 or larger
- JPG or PNG format
- No watermarks or promotional text overlaid on photos

### Posting on Google Business Profile

GBP Posts appear directly in your search listing and in the "Updates" tab. They expire after 7 days (standard posts) or when an event ends, so you need to post consistently.

- [ ] **Post frequency**: Minimum once per week; 2–3 times per week is ideal
- [ ] **What to post**: Weekly specials, new menu items, upcoming events, holiday hours, behind-the-scenes moments, team highlights, local partnerships
- [ ] **Post format**: 150–300 words with a high-quality photo and a call-to-action button (Order online, Book, Learn more)
- [ ] Set a recurring calendar reminder every Monday or Sunday to draft the week's GBP post
- [ ] Use "Event" post type for upcoming dinners, live music, prix fixe nights, or tastings — these show up on the listing with the event dates
- [ ] Use "Offer" post type for any limited-time promotions or discount events

### The Q&A Section (Use It Proactively)

The Q&A section on your GBP listing is editable — meaning you can ask AND answer your own questions before customers ask random ones. This is one of the most underused features in restaurant marketing.

- [ ] Log into GBP, navigate to your listing, and click "Ask a question" — then type common customer questions and answer them yourself
- [ ] **Questions to add proactively:**
  - "Do you take reservations?" (Answer: Yes/No — include link)
  - "Is there parking nearby?" (Answer: Describe street parking, lots, or valet)
  - "Do you have a gluten-free / vegan / vegetarian menu?" (Answer: Be specific)
  - "What is your dress code?" (Answer: Casual / Smart casual / No dress code)
  - "Do you have a private dining room for events?" (Answer: Yes/No + contact info)
  - "Is there a corkage fee if we bring our own wine?" (Answer if applicable)
  - "What are your hours on [major holiday]?" (Answer per holiday)
- [ ] Check Q&A weekly and respond to any new customer-submitted questions within 24 hours
- [ ] Upvote your own pre-populated answers so they appear at the top

### Enabling Google Messaging

- [ ] In your GBP dashboard, click "Messages" in the left sidebar
- [ ] Toggle messaging to "On"
- [ ] Set a welcome message (e.g., "Hi! Thanks for reaching out to [Restaurant Name]. We'll reply shortly. For reservations, you can also book at [link].")
- [ ] Assign a staff member to check and respond to messages daily — Google measures your response time and displays it publicly
- [ ] Target response time: under 24 hours; under 1 hour is ideal

## Section 2: Local SEO Basics for Restaurants

Search engine optimization for restaurants is simpler than it sounds. Your goal is to appear when someone searches for food in your area — and the way you do that is by making sure every online mention of your restaurant is consistent, keyword-rich, and spread across the right platforms.

### Keywords to Use in Your Bios and Posts

Forget trying to rank globally. You want to rank locally, which means combining what you serve with where you are.

**Formula: [Cuisine type] + [Neighborhood or City] + [Experience type]**

**Example keywords to build into your content:**
- "Italian restaurant in Andersonville Chicago"
- "Best brunch in Capitol Hill Seattle"
- "Farm-to-table dinner Williamsburg Brooklyn"
- "Dog-friendly patio restaurant Austin TX"
- "Romantic restaurants in Midtown Atlanta"
- "Happy hour downtown Portland"
- "Gluten-free friendly pizza Nashville"

**Where to use these keywords:**
- Your Instagram and Facebook bio
- The captions on your social media posts (especially the first line)
- Your Google Business Profile description
- Your website's homepage meta description and title tag
- Your Yelp and TripAdvisor profiles
- Alt text on food photos on your website

**How to mention your neighborhood naturally in posts:**

Instead of: "New spring menu available now."
Write: "Spring has finally arrived in Logan Square, and so has our new seasonal menu. Come find us at the corner of Kedzie and Milwaukee and taste what's fresh this week."

The goal is to sound natural while dropping the neighborhood or city name into your content consistently. Over time, Google reads these signals and associates your restaurant with that location.

### Citation Building: What It Is and Why It Matters

A **citation** is any online mention of your restaurant's name, address, and phone number (collectively called **NAP**). The more high-quality, consistent citations you have, the more Google trusts that your business is legitimate and well-established — which boosts your local search ranking.

**The core rule of citations: NAP must match exactly everywhere.**

- If your address is "1234 North Main Street," don't list it as "1234 N. Main St." on some sites and "1234 N Main Street" on others. Pick one format and use it everywhere.
- Your business name must be identical across all platforms — no abbreviations, no added keywords
- Your phone number format should be consistent: either (555) 867-5309 or 555-867-5309 — pick one style

### Platforms to Claim and How to Claim Them

Work through this list systematically over 2–3 weeks. Set aside 20–30 minutes per platform to complete the full profile.

- [ ] **Google Business Profile** — business.google.com (highest priority; done first)
- [ ] **Yelp** — biz.yelp.com — claim your existing listing or create one; add hours, photos, menu, and respond to reviews
- [ ] **TripAdvisor** — tripadvisor.com/owners — claim listing; upload 15+ photos; respond to all reviews
- [ ] **OpenTable** — restaurant.opentable.com — if you take reservations, get on OpenTable; it feeds Google's "Reserve a table" button
- [ ] **Resy** — resy.com/restaurant-partners — alternative to OpenTable, popular in urban markets
- [ ] **Apple Maps** — mapsconnect.apple.com — claim your listing so iPhone users find you; often overlooked
- [ ] **Foursquare / Swarm** — foursquare.com/business — still used for check-ins and location data; claim it
- [ ] **Bing Places** — bingplaces.com — Bing powers many voice search results; takes 10 minutes to set up
- [ ] **Facebook Business Page** — ensure your page has complete address, hours, and menu link; Facebook feeds local discovery
- [ ] **Zomato** — zomato.com — active in many cities; claim and complete your profile
- [ ] **Grubhub / DoorDash / Uber Eats** — if you offer delivery, complete these profiles fully with accurate menu and photos
- [ ] **Local newspaper / magazine restaurant guides** — search "[your city] best restaurants guide" and find any directory sites that accept submissions

**Audit your existing citations:**

Use a free tool like **Moz Local** (moz.com/local) or **BrightLocal** (brightlocal.com) to scan for existing mentions of your restaurant. These tools show you where your NAP information exists online and flag any inconsistencies. Run the audit, then go platform by platform to correct any mismatches.

## Section 3: Email List Building

Email marketing consistently delivers the highest return on investment of any marketing channel for restaurants — typically $36 for every $1 spent. Unlike social media, your email list is something you own outright. Algorithm changes and platform shutdowns cannot take it away from you.

### How to Collect Emails at the Table

**Method 1: Physical Sign-Up Card**
Place a small card at every table (3x4 inch cards work well). The card should say something like: "Join our VIP list — be the first to know about events, new menu items, and exclusive offers. [short URL or QR code to sign-up form]." Alternatively, have a paper sign-up sheet at the host stand.

**Method 2: QR Code**
Create a QR code that links directly to a simple email sign-up form (use Mailchimp's free form builder, ConvertKit, or a Google Form). Place the QR code on:
- Table tents
- The back of menus
- The check/receipt folder
- Near the exit
- On a small sign at the bar

**Method 3: Loyalty Card / Punch Card**
When a guest starts a punch card, write their name and email on it. Alternatively, use a simple digital loyalty tool (Stamp Me, Belly, or Square Loyalty) that captures the email at enrollment.

**Method 4: Ask at Checkout**
Train your POS staff with a simple script: "Would you like to join our email list? We send out our weekly specials and members get first access to our events." Most guests who had a good experience will say yes.

**Method 5: WiFi Login**
If you offer in-restaurant Wi-Fi, use a service like Beambox or Repeat Returns that captures an email address before granting Wi-Fi access. Guests opt in, you grow your list passively.

### Loyalty Program Basics

A loyalty program doesn't need to be complicated to work. The simplest programs outperform elaborate ones because customers actually use them.

**What makes a simple loyalty program work:**
- **One clear reward**: "Every 10 visits, get a free entree" — guests understand it immediately
- **Low barrier to start**: Give them credit for their first visit when they sign up, so they feel momentum
- **Visible progress**: Whether a punch card or an app, they need to see where they stand
- **Meaningful reward**: A free dessert feels weak; a free entree or a $15 credit feels worth coming back for
- **Staff buy-in**: Your team needs to ask about the card at every visit — build it into the checkout routine

### What to Send and How Often

**Weekly (best for active promotion):** Good for restaurants with changing specials, weekly events, or high visit frequency. Send every Tuesday or Wednesday — these days have higher open rates than Monday or Friday.

**Bi-weekly:** A sustainable middle ground. Rotating between "what's new" and "personal story/community" content keeps it fresh without overwhelming subscribers.

**Monthly:** Best if your content is more curated — a monthly newsletter with events, a featured dish deep dive, and community news. Lower unsubscribe rate but less top-of-mind presence.

**Event-triggered (always):** Regardless of your regular schedule, always send emails for:
- New seasonal menu launch
- Special events (wine dinners, holiday menus, live music series)
- Holiday closures or special hours
- A major announcement (new chef, renovation, anniversary)

Every new subscriber should automatically receive this sequence. Set it up once in Mailchimp, Klaviyo, or ConvertKit as an automated flow.

## Section 4: Influencer & Food Blogger Outreach

A single post from the right local food blogger can drive more reservations in one week than months of your own social media content. You don't need to partner with influencers who have millions of followers — local creators with 5,000 to 50,000 highly engaged followers in your city are often more effective.

### How to Find Local Food Bloggers

**Instagram searches to run:**
- Search the hashtag **#[yourcity]eats** (e.g., #chicagoeats, #austinfoodie)
- Search **#[yourneighborhood]food** or **#[yourneighborhood]restaurants**
- Search **#[yourcuisine][yourcity]** (e.g., #italianfoodchicago)
- Look at who's tagged in posts at your competitors — those are active food content creators in your market

**Google searches to find bloggers:**
- "[your city] food blog"
- "best food Instagram [your city]"
- "[your city] restaurant review blog"
- "[your cuisine] restaurant [your city] blog"

**Local Facebook groups:**
- Search "[Your City] Foodies" or "[Your City] Food & Drink" — bloggers and influencers frequently post in these groups
- Post a call: "Looking for local food content creators to collaborate with [Restaurant Name] — send me a DM if interested"

**What to look for before reaching out:**
- Posts photos that are high quality and authentic-feeling (not overly staged)
- Engagement rate above 2%: divide total likes + comments by total followers, multiply by 100
- Based in or frequently visits your city
- Their audience matches your target diner (check their other brand partnerships for clues)
- They've reviewed restaurants similar to yours in price point and cuisine

### Complete Outreach Email Template

**Subject line:** Collaboration at [Restaurant Name] — thought you'd love our [signature dish]

**Body:**

Hi [Creator's First Name],

I've been following your food content for a while — your [specific recent post or dish they covered] caught my attention, and I think your audience would genuinely love what we're doing at [Restaurant Name].

I'm [Your Name], the [owner/chef/marketing manager] here. We're a [brief description: e.g., "family-owned Thai restaurant in East Nashville specializing in regional dishes from Northern Thailand — not the standard Americanized menu you find elsewhere"].

I'd love to invite you in for a complimentary meal for two so you can experience the food firsthand. No pressure, no guaranteed post — I just think our [signature dish and 1–2 others] are worth your time, and I'd rather let the food speak for itself.

If you do enjoy the experience and feel like sharing it with your audience, I'd obviously be thrilled. We can also discuss a paid collaboration if that's how you prefer to work — I'm open to either approach.

Would you be open to visiting sometime in [month or timeframe]? I'm happy to accommodate your schedule.

Thank you for the incredible work you do spotlighting [city]'s food scene. Looking forward to potentially connecting.

Best,
[Your Name]
[Restaurant Name]
[Phone] | [Email]
[Website] | [Instagram handle]

### Comp Meal vs. Paid Partnership: When Each Makes Sense

**Comp meal (complimentary dinner) is appropriate when:**
- The creator has under 10,000 followers
- You're unsure if their content style fits your brand
- They primarily work on an exchange/review basis (many smaller creators do)
- You're building a relationship rather than a campaign
- The value of the meal ($60–$150) feels proportionate to their potential reach

**Paid partnership is appropriate when:**
- The creator has 10,000+ engaged local followers
- You want guaranteed deliverables (specific post, story, Reel) by a specific date
- You're running a campaign (new menu launch, reopening, event promotion)
- The creator explicitly works on a paid basis only
- You want usage rights to repurpose their content in your own marketing

**Fair rates for local food influencers:**
- 5K–10K followers: $50–$150 per post
- 10K–50K followers: $150–$500 per post
- 50K–100K followers: $500–$1,500 per post
- These rates typically include a single feed post + 1–3 Stories; negotiate accordingly

### How to Use UGC (User-Generated Content)

When a blogger or creator posts about you, that content is valuable beyond the original post — but you must handle it correctly.

**Getting permission:**
- Reply to their post: "This is beautiful! Would it be okay if we shared this on our Instagram with credit to you?"
- Alternatively, have a blanket statement on your "tag us" materials: "By tagging @[YourHandle], you grant us permission to share your content with full credit."
- For paid partnerships, include usage rights in your written agreement

**How to repurpose UGC:**
- Share their post to your Instagram Stories immediately with a tag and genuine reaction
- Repost (with credit) on your feed once a week if you have enough UGC
- Use their photos in your email newsletters (with permission and credit)
- Create a "Featured by" highlight on Instagram to collect all press and blogger features
- If you have a website gallery, add their photos there with credit

### Influencer Collaboration Dos and Don'ts

**Dos:**
- [ ] Do research the creator before reaching out — personalize every outreach email
- [ ] Do let them experience the restaurant organically — don't over-direct or hover during the meal
- [ ] Do follow up once (after 5–7 days) if you don't hear back from your initial outreach
- [ ] Do give creators a clear point of contact and your direct phone number for the visit
- [ ] Do confirm logistics in writing: date, time, number of guests, what's covered
- [ ] Do thank them after the visit whether or not they post

**Don'ts:**
- [ ] Don't ask for a specific number of posts or a guaranteed positive review from unpaid creators
- [ ] Don't make the creator feel watched or pressured during the meal
- [ ] Don't request edits to content after it's posted unless something is factually wrong
- [ ] Don't repost or use their content without explicit permission
- [ ] Don't invite multiple creators on the same evening without telling them — it can feel like an "influencer event" rather than a genuine experience
- [ ] Don't work with creators whose audience is predominantly outside your city or region — local reach is what matters

## Section 5: Neighborhood Partnerships

The businesses around you are not competitors — they're collaborators. Partnering with complementary local businesses lets you reach each other's customers, add value to both communities, and build a network that refers business to you consistently.

### Types of Complementary Businesses to Partner With

**Wine, beer, and spirits shops:** Perfect for co-hosted wine or cocktail pairing dinners. They bring expertise and their customer list; you bring food and ambiance. Both parties promote the event.

**Florists:** Before Valentine's Day, Mother's Day, and wedding season, cross-promote. A florist who offers "dinner reservations at [your restaurant] with every centerpiece order" turns a transactional purchase into an experience. You can offer a complimentary appetizer with any guest who mentions the florist.

**Event and wedding venues:** If there's a venue nearby that books private events, social dinners, or corporate gatherings, position yourself as their preferred caterer or "next-stop" for rehearsal dinners. Offer a referral fee or reciprocal arrangement.

**Hotels:** Nearby hotels are a pipeline of out-of-town guests who need dinner recommendations. Visit the hotel concierge, introduce yourself, bring sample menus, and leave a small stack of business cards or rack cards. Offer a "concierge courtesy" — a small gesture (free dessert, glass of wine) for guests who come in and mention the hotel recommendation. Concierges remember who takes care of their guests.

**Hair and nail salons (before date nights):** A salon that caters to date-night or special-occasion clients can refer customers to you. A flyer near their checkout — "Dinner plans? Reserve your table at [Restaurant Name]" — reaches people already in a celebratory mindset.

**Fitness studios and gyms:** Post-workout brunch partnerships, protein-forward menu callouts, or a "wellness Wednesday" dinner that highlights lighter menu options can align you with health-conscious locals.

**Corporate offices and coworking spaces:** Offer a "lunch for your team" package or host an occasional catered lunch. The decision-makers in those offices book holiday parties, client dinners, and team celebrations.

**Bookstores and record shops:** These attract culturally curious, community-minded customers who are the natural audience for a neighborhood restaurant. Hosting a book club dinner night or a vinyl listening dinner event creates a memorable cross-promotion.

**Body:**

[Describe the specific idea — e.g., "We feature your wines on our wine list and mention you in our email newsletter, and you keep a stack of our menus and a 'dinner pairing' recommendation near your register. When a customer buys a bottle we can recommend it be paired with one of our dishes."]

Would you be open to a quick 20-minute coffee to explore this? I'm happy to come to you.

Best,
[Your Name]
[Restaurant Name]
[Phone] | [Email]

### What to Offer in a Cross-Promotion

**Ideas for what each party gives:**

| What You Offer | What They Offer |
|---|---|
| Feature their product on your menu (wine, flowers, etc.) | Stack your menus or cards near their register |
| Mention them in your email newsletter | Mention you in their newsletter or social posts |
| Co-host a joint event at your space | Provide product/expertise for the event |
| Offer their customers a welcome gesture (free appetizer, dessert, first drink) | Offer your customers a discount or perk |
| Social media shoutout | Social media shoutout in return |

The key is **reciprocity and specificity** — vague "let's support each other" agreements fade. A specific offer ("we'll feature your roses as our table florals every Friday and credit you on Instagram; you'll hand out our reservation card with every bouquet sale") is something both parties will actually follow through on.

### How to Structure a Joint Event

A joint event is the highest-value form of neighborhood partnership. When done well, it brings both of your audiences together in one room and creates a memorable experience that people talk about.

**The basic structure:**
1. **Pick a format**: Wine dinner (you + wine shop), floral centerpiece dinner (you + florist), book club dinner (you + bookstore), craft cocktail workshop (you + spirits brand), date night bundle (you + salon or spa)
2. **Set a date**: Pick a typically slow night — Tuesday or Wednesday — so you fill seats that would otherwise sit empty
3. **Divide the duties**: Who handles promotion, ticketing, setup, and what each party provides
4. **Price it right**: Joint events should be ticketed in advance (Eventbrite or Tock are good platforms). Price to cover your food costs plus a profit margin — aim for ticket prices that reflect the full value of the experience
5. **Cap attendance**: Keep it intimate (20–40 seats) so it feels exclusive; scarcity drives demand
6. **Promote to both lists**: Each partner emails their own list, posts on their own social — you're doubling your reach with no extra ad spend
7. **Follow up after**: Collect attendee emails at the event; send a recap email to all attendees within 48 hours with photos and the date of the next event

## Section 6: Offline Marketing That Still Works

Digital marketing matters, but the tactile, in-person touchpoints at your restaurant are often where real loyalty is built. These offline tactics are simple to implement and generate returns every day your doors are open.

### Table Tent Cards

A table tent is a small folded card that stands upright on each table. It's an underused marketing tool with a captive audience — your current guests.

**What to put on a table tent:**
- Your social media handle with a QR code linking to your Instagram or Facebook page
- A prompt: "Follow us @[handle] for weekly specials and behind-the-scenes kitchen content"
- A QR code for your email list sign-up
- An upcoming event or special: "Reserve your spot for our New Year's Eve dinner" (update seasonally)
- A loyalty program sign-up prompt
- A review request: "Enjoyed your meal? Leave us a review on Google — it means the world to us" (with QR code linking directly to your GBP review page)

**Design tips:**
- Keep it simple — one or two messages per card, not six
- Use your brand colors and a food photo
- Make the QR code large enough to scan easily from across the table
- Print double-sided if you want two different messages
- Use cardstock (at minimum 100 lb cover weight) so they stand up and look professional

**QR code placement:**
- Bottom corner of the front face so it's visible without flipping the card
- Test every QR code with three different phones before printing

### Loyalty Punch Cards

The humble punch card is still one of the most effective loyalty tools for restaurants — primarily because guests can see their progress and carry it with them.

**Simple 10-visit punch card:**
- Front: Your logo, "VIP Loyalty Card," 10 punch boxes, and the reward (e.g., "Complete 10 visits — earn a FREE entree")
- Back: Your address, hours, website, and social handle
- Staff punch or stamp the card at the end of every visit
- The reward should feel meaningful: a free entree is better than a free side

**Digital punch card alternative:**
If you want to go digital without a full loyalty app investment, use **Stamp Me** (stampme.com) or **Loopy Loyalty** — both offer simple mobile punch cards for under $50/month.

**Key rules:**
- Never let a card expire (or set a minimum expiration of 1 year)
- Always offer the reward no-questions-asked when a guest completes a card
- Have cards at the host stand, the bar, and in check presenters so every staff member can offer them

### Birthday Club Setup

A birthday club is one of the highest-converting restaurant marketing tactics because the message arrives at exactly the right moment — when someone is already thinking about celebration.

**How to collect birthdays:**
- On your email sign-up form, add a field for "Birthday month and day" (not year — people are more comfortable sharing this)
- On loyalty card enrollment, ask for birthday month
- Add it to your loyalty program app or platform if you use one
- At the end of a positive dining experience, servers can say: "We have a birthday club — you'll get a special offer from us every year near your birthday. Want me to grab a sign-up card?"

**What to send:**
- Email #1: One week before their birthday. Subject: "Your birthday treat is waiting, [Name]" — offer a free dessert, complimentary glass of bubbly, or a birthday discount (20% off their birthday meal is compelling without being expensive)
- Email #2: On their birthday. Subject: "Happy Birthday from all of us at [Restaurant Name]!" — warm message, restate the offer, emphasize it's valid this week
- Email #3: One week after their birthday. Final reminder. Subject: "Last chance — your birthday gift expires soon." — urgency drives redemption

**What the offer should be:**
- Something meaningful but margin-friendly: free dessert, complimentary glass of wine or a signature cocktail, or 15–20% off the whole check
- Valid for a 2-week window around their birthday
- Non-transferable (the birthday guest must be present)

### Window Signage for Social Media

Your restaurant's exterior windows are free advertising to everyone who walks by. Use them.

**What to put on window signage:**
- "Follow us on Instagram: @[yourhandle]" with your handle in a large, easy-to-read font
- A QR code linking to your Instagram profile
- Optional: a sample food photo printed on the decal to visually pull people in

**Placement:**
- Eye level (between 4 and 6 feet from the ground) on a window near the entrance
- Should be visible from the sidewalk without having to look up or crouch
- Use removable vinyl decals — they look professional and don't damage windows
- Complement your storefront's overall aesthetic — don't let the signage look cluttered

**Where to order:**
- Vistaprint, Sticker Mule, or a local sign shop can produce window vinyl decals for $30–$80
- Canva has social media window signage templates that you can customize with your handle and colors

### Receipt and Check Marketing

The moment the check hits the table is a high-attention moment. Use it.

- **On the physical receipt**: Add a line at the bottom: "Thank you for dining with us! Leave us a Google review: [short URL or QR code]" — your POS system (Square, Toast, Clover) likely allows you to customize the receipt footer
- **In the check presenter**: Place a small card with your email sign-up QR code, loyalty program info, or upcoming event details
- **Server prompt**: Train servers to mention it naturally: "If you enjoyed your experience tonight, a Google or Yelp review really helps a small restaurant like ours. The QR code on your receipt takes you right there."

### "Tag Us" Prompts at the Table

Turning guests into content creators is free marketing with a personal endorsement. The key is making it easy and natural.

**How to prompt tagging:**
- Small card at the table: "Snap a photo and tag us @[handle] — we reshare our favorites!"
- Verbal: Train servers to mention it when delivering visually striking dishes: "This one's very 'gram-worthy — tag us if you get a shot!"
- A small printed sign at the bar or near a decorative element guests frequently photograph
- In the email footer: "Dining with us? Tag @[handle] — we'd love to see your experience."

**How to respond:**
- Check your tagged posts daily
- Like and comment on every tag — even a simple "So glad you enjoyed it! See you again soon 🍝" costs 10 seconds and builds loyalty
- Repost your best UGC to Stories within 24 hours of being tagged

## Section 7: 90-Day Local Marketing Plan

This plan takes you from foundation to momentum in three months. It is realistic, week-by-week, and designed for an owner-operator who is short on time. Assign each task to a specific person and put the dates in your calendar.

---

### Month 1: Build the Foundation

The goal of Month 1 is to get every basic system working: your Google profile is complete, your email list has a way to grow, and your team is briefed. You are not trying to go viral. You are building the infrastructure that makes everything else possible.

**Week 1: Google Business Profile Sprint**
- [ ] Day 1: Claim and verify your Google Business Profile if not already done
- [ ] Day 2: Complete every field in the profile (hours, description, categories, website, menu link, booking link)
- [ ] Day 3: Upload at least 15 photos across all categories (exterior, interior, food, team)
- [ ] Day 4: Populate the Q&A section with 8–10 pre-written questions and answers
- [ ] Day 5: Enable messaging; write your welcome auto-reply
- [ ] Day 6: Write and publish your first GBP post (a weekly special or current menu highlight)
- [ ] Day 7: Audit your NAP consistency across Google, Yelp, and Facebook — fix any mismatches

**Week 2: Email Infrastructure**
- [ ] Set up a free Mailchimp account and create an email list
- [ ] Design a simple sign-up form with fields for First Name, Email, and Birthday (month/day)
- [ ] Generate a QR code linking to your sign-up form
- [ ] Design table tent cards featuring the QR code — order print run
- [ ] Write the 3-email welcome sequence (use the templates in Section 3 as your starting point)
- [ ] Set up the automation so new subscribers receive all three emails on the correct schedule
- [ ] Brief your staff: explain the email list, why it matters, and how to invite guests to join

**Week 3: Citation Building**
- [ ] Claim/complete your Yelp listing (photos, hours, menu, description)
- [ ] Claim/complete your TripAdvisor listing
- [ ] Claim your Apple Maps listing via mapsconnect.apple.com
- [ ] Claim your Bing Places listing
- [ ] Verify your Facebook Business page is complete with accurate address and hours
- [ ] Run a Moz Local or BrightLocal citation audit; note the platforms flagging inconsistencies

**Week 4: Offline Materials and Staff Briefing**
- [ ] Table tent cards should arrive this week — place one on every table
- [ ] Print loyalty punch cards; place a stack at host stand, bar, and in check presenters
- [ ] Set up the birthday club: add birthday field to your sign-up form if not done; write birthday emails
- [ ] Order window vinyl with your Instagram handle
- [ ] Brief all front-of-house staff on: loyalty card, email sign-up QR code, "tag us" prompt, and how to ask for Google reviews
- [ ] Post the "Follow us on Instagram" window vinyl

**Month 1 Review:**
By the end of Week 4, you should have: a complete GBP, an email sign-up system in place with 3-email automation running, 5+ citations claimed and consistent, and all offline materials deployed. Count how many new email subscribers you collected in Month 1.

---

### Month 2: Build Community

The goal of Month 2 is to connect with your neighborhood — both online and in person. You are reaching out to potential partners, connecting with local creators, and starting to send emails consistently.

**Week 5: First Email and Content Batch**
- [ ] Send your first email newsletter to your growing list (introduce the newsletter, feature a dish, tease something coming in Month 2)
- [ ] Spend 2 hours batching content for the month: photograph 10–15 dishes, prep 8–12 caption drafts
- [ ] Schedule 3 GBP posts for the month using your batched content
- [ ] Set up a recurring Monday morning GBP post reminder in your calendar

**Week 6: Influencer Outreach**
- [ ] Identify 10–15 local food Instagram accounts using the search methods in Section 4
- [ ] Create a simple spreadsheet: creator name, handle, follower count, estimated engagement rate, email, outreach status
- [ ] Draft personalized outreach emails to your top 8 creators
- [ ] Send the first batch of outreach (5 emails); send remaining 3 by midweek
- [ ] Follow each creator on Instagram and engage genuinely with their content this week

**Week 7: Neighborhood Partnership Outreach**
- [ ] List 5 complementary local businesses you'd most like to partner with
- [ ] Write 5 personalized partnership pitch emails using the template in Section 5
- [ ] Send the emails; follow up with a personal visit if email doesn't get a response within 5 days
- [ ] Book your first joint event — aim for it to take place during Month 3
- [ ] Set up a simple tracking sheet: partner business, proposed idea, status, follow-up date

**Week 8: Community Visibility**
- [ ] Join 2–3 local Facebook groups (neighborhood groups, foodie groups, parent groups) and participate genuinely — introduce your restaurant where appropriate
- [ ] Ask one satisfied regular customer to leave a Google review (verbally or via text follow-up)
- [ ] Respond to every Google and Yelp review from the past 6 months that you haven't responded to yet
- [ ] Post a GBP post about an upcoming event or special to drive foot traffic

**Month 2 Review:**
By the end of Month 2, you should have sent 2 email newsletters, responded to all outstanding reviews, launched outreach to 8+ creators, confirmed at least 1 partnership conversation, and seen a measurable uptick in GBP profile views.

---

### Month 3: Create Growth

The goal of Month 3 is to build on everything you've set up. You're hosting your first joint event, getting creator visits in your restaurant, and refining what's working.

**Week 9: Creator Visits and Content**
- [ ] Follow up on any creator outreach from Week 6 that hasn't responded (one follow-up email only)
- [ ] Welcome and schedule any creators who have accepted your invitation
- [ ] If a creator has visited and posted, reshare their content; send a thank-you note or email
- [ ] Photograph their visit (with permission) and create content about the collaboration
- [ ] Send email #2 to your list this month — theme: a behind-the-scenes kitchen story or supplier spotlight

**Week 10: Joint Event Execution**
- [ ] Finalize all logistics for your first joint event (date, menu, ticketing, promotion)
- [ ] Both businesses send email newsletters announcing the event to their respective lists
- [ ] Both businesses post about the event on social media (3+ posts each in the week before)
- [ ] Set up a sign-in or email capture at the event door
- [ ] Host the event; photograph it for future marketing use

**Week 11: Review and Double Down**
- [ ] Send a recap email to your full list and to event attendees separately
- [ ] Review your GBP Insights: how many views, direction requests, calls, and website clicks this month vs. Month 1?
- [ ] Review email open rates (target: 25%+ open rate is healthy for restaurants)
- [ ] Identify which tactics drove the most measurable results — more email sign-ups? More covers on a specific night?
- [ ] Plan Month 4: which partnerships to deepen, which creators to invite back, what events to schedule next quarter

**Week 12: Systems and Sustainability**
- [ ] Audit all citation platforms: have all been claimed and are all NAP entries consistent?
- [ ] Check your Google review count: set a goal for where you want to be in 90 more days
- [ ] Document what's working in a simple one-page "marketing summary" — just a running list of what drove results
- [ ] Brief staff again on loyalty card, email sign-up, and review requests — make these habits permanent
- [ ] Write and schedule next month's email content and GBP posts

**Month 3 Review:**
By the end of Month 3, you should have: hosted one community event, received at least one creator visit, grown your email list meaningfully, and established habits (weekly GBP posts, biweekly emails, monthly partnership check-in) that will carry you forward sustainably.
