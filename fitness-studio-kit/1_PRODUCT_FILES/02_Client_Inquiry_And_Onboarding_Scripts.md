# Client Inquiry & Onboarding System

## How to Use This System

Every new member relationship begins before someone ever walks through your door. The moment a prospect reaches out — whether by email, text, or DM — the clock starts on their first impression of your studio. Research consistently shows that responding within the first hour dramatically increases the likelihood that a prospect converts to a paying member. When someone inquires about your studio, they are often reaching out to two or three competitors at the same time, and the first studio to respond thoughtfully and warmly is almost always the one that earns their business. These templates are designed to help you respond quickly without sacrificing the personalized feel that boutique fitness studios are known for. Before you send any message, take ten seconds to add the prospect's name and reference their specific stated goal — this small act of personalization signals to them that they are not just a transaction, and it builds the trust that leads to long-term membership.

Onboarding is not a formality — it is the single greatest lever you have for reducing early dropout. The first two to four weeks of a new member's journey are the most fragile. If someone feels confused, overlooked, or unsure whether they belong, they will quietly stop showing up before you even realize they have gone. A structured onboarding sequence — welcome email, day-before reminder, post-first-class follow-up, and a two-week check-in — creates the kind of attentive, human experience that keeps new members coming back long enough to form a habit. Use the templates in this system as starting points, not word-for-word scripts. Every prospect is different, and your voice as a studio owner or front desk team member should come through in every message. The goal is to sound like a knowledgeable friend who genuinely wants to help, not like a fitness corporation following a sales process.

## SECTION 2 — CONSULTATION / DISCOVERY CALL SCRIPT

---

### Pre-Call Preparation Checklist

- Review any intake form or inquiry email the prospect submitted before the call, and highlight their stated goal and any specific language they used to describe it — you'll want to echo that language back to them during the call.
- Note their name and any personal details they shared (their city, their job, their kids, their injury) so you can reference them naturally and make the conversation feel personal rather than generic.
- Check whether this person has taken a trial class or visited the studio before — if they have, pull any notes from that visit and start the call by acknowledging their experience.
- Prepare for the three most common objections — price, schedule, and "I need to think about it" — so you are not caught off guard and can respond with genuine empathy rather than panic.
- Have your current membership options and pricing pulled up in front of you, along with any current promotions or new member specials you can mention if the moment is right.
- Write down two or three specific program features that are most relevant to this person's stated goal, so you can speak to them specifically rather than running through a generic list.
- Set up a quiet, distraction-free space for the call — close unnecessary browser tabs, silence your phone notifications, and have a notepad ready to write down key things they say.
- Have your calendar or booking link open and ready to pull up immediately if they want to schedule a first class or follow-up session at the end of the call.
- Review any previous communication this person has had with your studio — emails, DMs, front-desk notes — so you are not asking them to repeat themselves.
- Set a mental intention to listen more than you talk. Your goal in this call is to understand what they need, not to sell them something.

### Discovery Questions

**1. "What made you reach out to us specifically, right now?"**
This question reveals what triggered the inquiry and what the prospect's emotional state is — whether they are in crisis mode (injury, weight gain, low energy) or in aspiration mode (new year, new chapter). Use this answer to understand urgency and to connect your studio's offering to the specific moment they are in.

**2. "How would you describe your current relationship with exercise — are you someone who's been consistent, getting back into it, or just starting fresh?"**
This tells you where to pitch the conversation. A returning exerciser needs a different onboarding experience than a complete beginner, and acknowledging their level accurately builds immediate trust. Use this to frame your program recommendation appropriately.

**3. "What is the single most important outcome you want from joining a studio like ours?"**
Asking for the single most important outcome forces the prospect to prioritize, which reveals what they truly care about versus what they think they should care about. This is the anchor you will return to throughout the rest of the call.

**4. "Have you tried other gyms, studios, or fitness programs in the past? What was your experience?"**
This uncovers both their fitness history and any negative experiences that may be creating hesitation. If they had a bad experience somewhere, they are likely carrying that bias into this conversation, and knowing about it allows you to proactively address it.

**5. "What got in the way of staying consistent in the past?"**
This is one of the most valuable questions you can ask because it surfaces the real barriers — not the ones people say publicly, but the ones that actually caused them to stop. Use the answer to explain how your studio's structure, community, or accountability model specifically addresses that barrier.

**6. "On a scale of one to ten, how important is it to you right now to make a real change — and what would need to be true for it to be a ten?"**
This question comes from motivational interviewing and is remarkably effective at helping prospects articulate their own commitment level. Their answer reveals whether they are truly ready to invest, and the "what would make it a ten" follow-up helps them identify and voice their own barriers — far more powerful than you identifying barriers for them.

**7. "What does your week typically look like? Do you have any sense of when you'd realistically be able to fit sessions in?"**
Scheduling is one of the most common reasons people quit or never start. Getting specific about when they can actually show up helps you recommend a schedule that is realistic, not aspirational, which dramatically improves follow-through.

**8. "Is there anyone else in your life who is supportive of you doing this — a partner, a friend, a coworker?"**
Social support is one of the strongest predictors of fitness habit formation. This question also tells you whether there is a decision-making partner you need to account for (a spouse who holds the purse strings, for example) and whether referral or bring-a-friend programs might be relevant.

**9. "Have you ever worked with a personal trainer or attended group fitness classes before? What did you think?"**
This helps you understand their comfort level with the format you offer and reveals any specific resistance — some people hate group settings, some feel intimidated by one-on-one coaching. Use this to address those preferences directly when presenting your programs.

**10. "What would success look like for you six months from now?"**
Future-pacing is a powerful tool — asking someone to envision their success helps them feel ownership over the outcome, which increases their motivation to commit. It also gives you a concrete, emotionally resonant vision you can reference when presenting your program.

**11. "Is budget something we should talk about openly so I can make sure I'm recommending the right option for you?"**
Rather than dancing around price, this question invites an honest conversation about budget early, which saves time for both parties and allows you to present the most relevant option rather than leading with your most expensive offer. Most people appreciate the directness.

**12. "What would need to happen on this call today for you to feel confident about taking a next step?"**
This closing discovery question is transformative because it hands the prospect control of their own decision-making process. Their answer tells you exactly what they need from you in the next ten minutes — whether it's more information, a specific guarantee, a particular price point, or simply a clear description of what to expect on day one.

---

### Presenting Your Programs

"Based on everything you've shared with me, I want to make sure I'm recommending something that actually fits where you are right now — not where you want to be eventually, but what's going to set you up for success in the first 30 days. You mentioned that [restate their primary goal in their own words] is really the priority, and you also said that [restate their key barrier, e.g., 'staying consistent has been hard when life gets busy'] has gotten in the way before. I want to speak to both of those things.

What I'd recommend for someone in your position is [Program Name], and here's why I'm saying that specifically. [Describe the program.] The reason this fits your goal of [their stated goal] is because [specific program feature that addresses that goal]. And when it comes to [their stated barrier], this program solves for that by [specific structural element — e.g., the flexibility to book same-day, the accountability check-ins, the small class size that means the instructor knows if you miss].

We also have [second option] which is great if [alternative scenario] — but based on what you told me, I honestly think [first program] is the stronger fit. I'm not going to oversell you on more than you need, and I'm not going to undersell you on something that won't get you to where you said you want to be. Does this feel like it aligns with what you were picturing? [Pause and let them respond.] What questions do you have for me?"

---

### Handling the Price Objection

**"I need to think about it."**

"Of course, and I completely respect that — this is a real financial decision and it makes total sense to sit with it. I just want to make sure I haven't left any questions unanswered that might be making it harder to decide. Is there something specific you're weighing, or something I didn't cover well enough? [Listen.] I ask because in my experience, when someone says they need to think about it, there's usually one particular thing that feels unresolved — and I'd rather address that now than have you sitting on it all week. If it turns out it's just about time, that's completely fine — I'll follow up with you on [specific day], and if you have questions before then, please reach out directly. I want to make sure this feels right for you."

**"It's a bit more than I was expecting."**

"I hear you, and I appreciate you being honest with me about that — it helps me make sure I'm recommending the right thing. Can I ask what you were expecting it to be? [Listen.] Got it. I want to be transparent with you about what's included because I think when people compare our membership to a larger gym or a class pass app, the value looks really different. [Briefly explain what is included — small class sizes, instructor attention, community, results support, etc.] That said, I'm not interested in putting you in something that stretches you too thin — if the unlimited membership feels like too much right now, we do have [lower-tier option] which might be a better starting point and there's nothing stopping you from upgrading once you see how often you're coming. Would that feel more comfortable?"

**"I can't afford it right now."**

"Thank you for telling me that directly — I genuinely appreciate it. I'm not going to pressure you or try to talk you into something that doesn't make financial sense for your life right now, because that's not a good foundation for a relationship. What I will say is this: if the goal you described — [restate their goal] — is truly important to you, there may be a version of this that works within your current budget. We do have [lowest-tier option or intro offer] which gives you access to [specific benefit] at a more accessible price point, and that might be a way to get started without overcommitting. And if the timing genuinely isn't right, that's okay too — the door is open whenever it is. Would it be helpful if I sent you the pricing breakdown in writing so you have it when things shift?"

---

### Closing the Call

"I really enjoyed this conversation — thank you for taking the time and for being so open with me. I feel like I have a much better sense of where you're at and what would actually serve you well.

[If they are ready to join:] So the next step I'd recommend is [specific action — booking their first class, completing their sign-up online, coming in for an orientation session]. I can walk you through that right now if you'd like, or I can send you a direct link that makes it really simple. Which would you prefer?

[If they need more time:] I completely understand — there's no rush. What I'll do is send you a follow-up email in the next hour with a summary of what we talked about, the program I'd recommend, and all the details so you have everything in one place. Does [specific day] work for me to follow up briefly to see if you have any other questions? [Confirm the follow-up date.] And in the meantime, please don't hesitate to reach out to me directly — I'd much rather answer a question than have you sitting with uncertainty.

Either way, I'm genuinely rooting for you. What you described — [restate their goal] — is absolutely achievable, and we would be honored to be part of that journey. Take care, and I hope we see you soon."

### Cancellation Exit Survey Questions

**1. "What was the primary reason you decided to cancel your membership?"**
This is the most direct question on the survey and should be presented as multiple choice with an "other" option — common answers include cost, scheduling, moving, health reasons, and not getting the results they hoped for. The distribution of answers across this question over time tells you which problem areas require the most attention in your business.

**2. "Was there a specific moment, experience, or interaction that contributed to your decision to leave?"**
This open-ended question is where your most actionable feedback lives. A member may have been quietly disappointed by something — a scheduling change, a negative experience with a specific instructor, feeling overlooked — that you had no idea about. Even one honest answer here can prevent the same experience from affecting future members.

**3. "How satisfied were you with the results you experienced during your time with us?"**
This question reveals whether your programming is delivering on its promise. Members who cancel while feeling satisfied with their results are very different from members who cancel because they saw no progress — the former are likely leaving for logistical reasons (cost, schedule, relocation) and are strong candidates for a future return, while the latter signal a gap in your coaching or programming quality.

**4. "Was there anything we could have done differently that might have changed your decision to leave?"**
This question invites members to give you a specific, actionable alternative — and it also implicitly asks them to reflect on whether the departure was avoidable. Some members will surprise themselves when they realize there was a solution available they hadn't considered, and this question has occasionally turned a cancellation into a pause or a rejoining.

**5. "How likely are you to return to [Studio Name] in the future, and is there anything we should know to make that easier for you?"**
This forward-looking question distinguishes permanent cancellations from temporary departures, which are extremely common in the fitness industry. A member who says they are likely to return but is moving for six months is a completely different situation from a member who had a deeply negative experience. Tracking this answer over time also gives you a meaningful metric: a high "likely to return" score among canceling members indicates strong brand affinity even among those who leave.
