# Local Marketing & Community Growth Playbook for Fitness Studios

## How to Use This Playbook

Going viral isn't a strategy. Being the go-to fitness studio in your neighborhood is. This playbook focuses on the tactics that build sustainable local visibility: showing up in Google search when someone types "yoga near me," getting email addresses from people who've already tried you, and building the community relationships that turn one-time visitors into lifelong members.

Use this alongside your content calendar. The content gets attention; this playbook turns that attention into actual memberships.

---

## SECTION 1 — GOOGLE BUSINESS PROFILE FOR FITNESS STUDIOS

### Complete Optimization Checklist

Work through every item below. Incomplete profiles rank lower and convert worse. Set aside 90 minutes to complete this in one sitting.

**1. Claim and verify your listing**
Go to business.google.com and search for your business name. If it already exists, claim it. If it doesn't, create it from scratch. Verification typically happens via postcard (5-7 days), phone call, or video verification. Do not skip this step — unverified listings can be edited by anyone and may be suppressed in local search results.

**2. Business name — exact legal match**
Enter your name exactly as it appears on your physical signage and legal documents. Do not add keyword phrases like "best yoga studio" or "Austin's #1 pilates." Google penalizes keyword stuffing in business names and can suspend your listing. Your name is your name.

**3. Primary category — choose the most accurate one**
- If you primarily teach yoga classes: Yoga Studio
- If you offer general group fitness and equipment: Gym
- If you offer one-on-one training: Personal Trainer
- If you primarily teach pilates (reformer or mat): Pilates Studio
- If you offer multiple modalities equally: choose the one that drives the most revenue and is most searchable in your market

The primary category has the greatest weight in local ranking. Pick one and commit to it.

**4. Additional categories**
Add up to 9 additional categories to capture secondary searches. Common additions:
- Fitness Center
- Dance Studio (if applicable)
- Meditation Center (if applicable)
- Barre Studio (if applicable)
- Physical Fitness Program
- Sports Club

Only add categories that genuinely describe what you offer. Irrelevant categories can hurt your relevance signals.

**5. Address — enter it exactly as the post office has it**
Use your official mailing address. If you're a personal trainer who trains clients at a shared location or in-home, you can hide your address and set a service area instead. NAP consistency (Name, Address, Phone) across all online directories is a local SEO ranking factor — what you enter here must match your website footer, Yelp listing, and every other directory exactly.

**6. Phone number**
Use a local phone number, not a 1-800 or Google Voice number. Local numbers are a trust signal. Make sure this number is the same on your website and all directories.

**7. Website URL**
Link to your homepage or, if you have a booking page, link directly to it. Ensure the page loads quickly on mobile. A broken or slow link damages your profile.

**8. Hours — complete and current**
Enter your full weekly hours. Use the "Special Hours" feature to set holiday hours in advance — studios that don't update for holidays frustrate potential visitors and signal inactivity to Google. Schedule a calendar reminder in November to update holiday hours for the entire December-January period.

**9. Description (150-300 words, no links or HTML)**
Your description should do three things: tell people what you offer, tell them who it's for, and tell them what makes you different. Write it in plain conversational language. Work in 3-5 naturally placed keywords (your city, neighborhood, and class type). Here is a template:

"[Studio Name] is [City]'s [boutique / women-only / family-friendly / results-focused] [yoga studio / pilates studio / personal training studio]. We offer [specific class types: hot yoga, reformer pilates, HIIT, etc.] for [who: beginners, athletes, busy professionals, postpartum moms, etc.].

Unlike [big box gyms / online-only programs], [Studio Name] keeps class sizes small so [instructors / coaches] can focus on your form and goals. Our instructors are certified in [certifications] and average [X] years of teaching experience.

Located in [Neighborhood], [Studio Name] is minutes from [local landmark or cross street]. We're the studio [City] residents turn to when they want [specific outcome: to de-stress, to get stronger, to recover from injury, to finally feel at home in a fitness class].

New? Try your first class free / for $[X]. Book online at [website] or call us at [phone]. We'd love to meet you."

**10. Photos — minimum quantities and types**
Photos are one of the most important factors in converting a profile view into a visit. Listings with more than 100 photos receive dramatically more calls and direction requests than the average. Shoot for these minimums:

- Exterior (3+ photos): Front of building, parking area, signage visible. Shows people they have the right place.
- Interior — studio space (10+ photos): Empty studio at different angles, equipment, lighting. Shows the environment.
- Classes in action (15+ photos): Real classes with real members, instructor adjustments, full class energy. Shows what the experience feels like.
- Team photos (5+ photos): Headshots and action shots of each instructor. Builds trust.
- Equipment close-ups (3+ photos): Reformers, weights, props, specialty equipment. Shows investment in quality.
- Reception / front desk (2+ photos): Clean, welcoming. Sets expectations.

Upload new photos at least once a month. Google favors active listings.

**11. Q&A — pre-populate 8 questions with your own answers**
Google's Q&A section is public and anyone can post questions or answers, including competitors. Get ahead of it by posting the questions yourself and answering them. Log out, ask the question from a personal Google account, then log back in as the business owner and answer. See the Q&A section below for all 8 questions written out in full.

**12. Services section — list your classes and programs**
Use the Services feature to list every class type and program you offer. Include the name, a 1-2 sentence description, and a price or price range. This appears directly in your profile and helps Google understand what searches to show you for.

Example entries:
- Beginner Yoga: "A slow-paced introduction to yoga postures and breathwork. Perfect for those new to yoga or returning after a break. $18 drop-in or included with membership."
- Reformer Pilates: "Small-group reformer sessions with a maximum of 6 participants. Focuses on core stability, flexibility, and postural alignment. $35 drop-in."
- Personal Training: "One-on-one training sessions with a certified personal trainer. Custom programming for weight loss, strength, or athletic performance. From $75 per session."

**13. Review link — save and distribute it**
Get your shareable review link by going to your Google Business dashboard, clicking "Ask for reviews," and copying the direct link. Shorten it with bit.ly or use a QR code generator so it's easy to share. Test it from a different device before using it anywhere.

**14. Booking button**
Under "Booking" in your profile settings, link directly to your online booking page (Mindbody, Acuity, Pike13, etc.). This adds a "Book" button to your Google profile that reduces the steps between intent and action. This button directly increases conversions.

**15. Post cadence — minimum 1x per week**
Google Business posts expire after 7 days (offers last longer). Posting weekly keeps your profile active and gives Google fresh signals. See the Posting section below for what to post.

**16. Messaging — turn it on**
Enable the messaging feature so potential members can text your business directly from Google. Respond within a few hours. Google tracks your response rate and may disable messaging if you're slow to respond.

**17. Attributes — set them all**
Under "More" in your profile, you'll find attributes: wheelchair accessible, LGBTQ+ friendly, women-owned, veteran-owned, Black-owned, etc. Set every attribute that applies. These appear as badges on your profile and help people self-select.

### Photo Guide for Fitness Studios

You do not need a professional photographer to take compelling Google Business photos. A modern smartphone, good lighting, and intentional composition will produce photos that convert. Here is how to approach each category.

**Exterior photos**
Take these during daylight, mid-morning for even light. Get three angles: straight-on facade showing signage, a wider shot showing the building in context (parking, surrounding street), and a close-up of the front door. If you have window graphics or a chalkboard sign, photograph those separately. These photos answer the question: "Will I recognize it when I get there?"

**Interior — empty studio**
Shoot on a day with clean equipment, no clutter, and good lighting. Open all blinds. Turn on all studio lights. Take wide-angle shots from each corner. Get the ceiling if it's visually interesting (exposed beams, skylights). Photograph prop walls or equipment storage — it shows organization and investment. Use portrait mode to blur backgrounds slightly if your space has unavoidable clutter. These photos answer: "Is this a place I'll feel comfortable?"

**Classes in action**
This is your most important photo category. Ask 2-3 enthusiastic members if you can photograph them during class (get written permission if you plan to use images in marketing). Shoot during a class that's at least 70% full — an empty studio looks uninviting. Capture: the instructor cueing the group, a close-up of a member in a strong pose, the instructor giving a hands-on adjustment, the group mid-flow. Shoot from different heights (low angle, standing, from the back of the room). These photos answer: "Will I belong here?"

**Team photos**
Each instructor should have: a clean headshot (neutral or studio background), an action shot teaching, and an informal candid. Coordinated outfit colors (all in black, or your brand color) make the team look cohesive without requiring expensive styling. Natural light near a window is flattering and free.

**Equipment**
Close-up, well-lit shots of reformers, props, specialty equipment. Show clean, maintained gear. These photos answer: "Is the equipment quality?"

**Photo refresh schedule**
Update with new photos monthly. Good ongoing opportunities: seasonal decor, special events, new equipment, visiting instructors, community events. Google notices when profiles get new uploads and may reward them with a temporary ranking boost.

### Posting on Google Business

Google Business posts work like a mini blog that lives directly in your search listing. Posts expire after 7 days (events and offers have different rules), so consistency matters more than perfection.

**What to post each week (rotate through these four formats):**

Week A — Class Spotlight: Feature one class with a photo, 2-3 sentences about what to expect, who it's for, and a link to book. Example: "Our Wednesday 6pm Power Yoga is filling up fast. This 60-minute all-levels flow is set to upbeat music and designed to release the stress of the workday. Spots available — book via the link in our profile."

Week B — Member Win: With permission, share a member achievement. "Congratulations to [First Name], who just completed her 50th class with us. We're so proud of her consistency. What's your milestone? Tag us when you hit it."

Week C — New or Seasonal Program: Announce or re-promote a program that's starting soon. Include start date, what it involves, who it's for, and how to sign up.

Week D — Tip or Educational Content: One practical fitness or wellness tip related to what you teach. 3-4 sentences. No selling. Positions you as an authority.

**How to repurpose Instagram content in 30 seconds:**
Open your Instagram post. Copy the caption text. Open your Google Business profile. Click "Add update." Paste the caption, trim it to 1,500 characters if needed (Google's limit), and add the photo from your camera roll. Done. The formats are compatible — just remove hashtags from the Google post since they don't function the same way there.

**Post with an offer:**
Use the "Offer" post type for promotions (intro deals, referral programs, limited-time pricing). These display more prominently in the profile and include a button. Set an expiration date to create urgency.

## SECTION 2 — LOCAL SEO FOR FITNESS STUDIOS

### Keywords to Use in Your Bio, Website, and Posts

Local SEO for fitness businesses is straightforward: you want to show up when someone in your city or neighborhood searches for what you offer. The following strategies and examples work across your Google profile, website, and social media bio.

**Strategy 1 — City + service type**
Format: "[City] yoga studio," "[City] pilates studio," "[City] personal trainer"
Usage: Use this format in your Google Business description, website homepage H1 heading, and Instagram bio. It is the highest-value keyword combination because it directly matches how most people search. Example bio placement: "Austin yoga studio specializing in hot yoga and slow flow."

**Strategy 2 — Neighborhood + service type**
Format: "[Neighborhood] gym," "[Neighborhood] fitness studio," "yoga in [Neighborhood]"
Usage: If your city is large, neighborhood keywords can help you rank for more specific, less competitive searches. Someone who lives in East Nashville searching "East Nashville yoga" is more likely to convert than someone searching "Nashville yoga" from across town. Use the neighborhood name on your website's About page, in alt text for location photos, and in Google Business posts.

**Strategy 3 — Near me intent**
Format: "pilates near me [city]," "yoga classes near me"
Usage: You cannot literally put "near me" in your content — Google determines "near me" results by physical proximity. But you can support these rankings by ensuring your Google Business Profile is complete, your NAP is consistent, and you're getting regular reviews. The more signals Google has that you're a legitimate, active local business, the more often you'll appear for near-me searches.

**Strategy 4 — Demographic-specific search**
Format: "women's only gym [city]," "prenatal yoga [city]," "yoga for beginners [city]," "yoga for seniors [city]," "kids yoga [city]"
Usage: If you serve a specific demographic, use those terms in your class descriptions on your website and in your Google Business services section. Someone searching "prenatal yoga Austin" has extremely high purchase intent — they're looking for something specific. If you offer it, you need to say so explicitly in text that Google can read.

**Strategy 5 — Goal-oriented search**
Format: "yoga for weight loss [city]," "pilates for back pain [city]," "strength training for women [city]"
Usage: These searches come from people who have already decided what problem they want to solve. Use goal language in your class descriptions, Google Business posts, and FAQ sections. Example: "Our Reformer Pilates classes in [city] are especially popular with members dealing with lower back pain or recovering from injury."

**Strategy 6 — Time-based search**
Format: "early morning yoga [city]," "lunchtime pilates [city]," "evening fitness classes [city]"
Usage: Working professionals search by schedule, not just by service. Mention your class times on your Google profile, in posts, and in your website metadata. "We offer 5:30am and 6am yoga classes in [city] for members who want to start their workday right."

**Strategy 7 — Certification and specialty**
Format: "RYT 500 yoga teacher [city]," "NASM certified personal trainer [city]," "pre/postnatal yoga [city]"
Usage: These are lower-volume but very high-intent searches. Include certifications and specialties in instructor bios on your website. This also builds trust with prospective members who are evaluating quality.

**Strategy 8 — Studio type and experience**
Format: "boutique fitness studio [city]," "hot yoga studio [city]," "reformer pilates studio [city]," "small group training [city]"
Usage: "Boutique" and "small group" signal a premium, personalized experience. Use these modifiers in your website and Google Business description to differentiate yourself from large commercial gyms.

**Strategy 9 — Comparative and alternative searches**
Format: "yoga alternative to [big box gym] [city]," "more personal than franchise fitness [city]"
Note: Do not use specific competitor names in paid ads without legal review. In organic content, you can reference being "an alternative to big-box gym memberships" or "a more personal alternative to franchise fitness studios" without naming specific competitors. This captures comparison searches from people who are already evaluating their options.

**Important — avoid keyword stuffing:**
Do not write: "Austin yoga studio Austin yoga classes Austin yoga teacher Austin yoga near me Austin." This reads as spam and Google may suppress your listing or page. Instead, use each keyword variant once, naturally, in a complete sentence. The goal is readable content that happens to contain relevant terms — not a list of terms.

### The 5 Most Important Directories for Fitness Businesses

**1. Google Business Profile**
Why it matters: The single highest-impact local listing. Controls how you appear in Google Search and Google Maps. Nearly half of all Google searches are local. New members looking for a studio in your city will encounter your Google profile before they reach your website.
How to claim: business.google.com. Search for your business. Claim it or create it. Complete verification.
What to optimize: Everything in the checklist above. Prioritize: categories, description, photos, Q&A, and weekly posting.

**2. Yelp**
Why it matters: Yelp has significant authority in local search and often appears in the top 5 results for "[business type] [city]" searches, even if you haven't actively managed your Yelp page. A neglected Yelp page with no photos and old information still shows up — better to own it.
How to claim: biz.yelp.com. Search for your business and claim it. Verification via phone or email.
What to optimize: Business name (exact match), category (Yoga, Gyms, Pilates, etc.), location, phone, hours, photos (use the same set from your Google Business shoot), a 250-word description. Respond to every review — both positive and negative. Yelp's algorithm rewards engaged business owners.
Note on reviews: Never ask customers to post a Yelp review in bulk — Yelp penalizes review solicitation. Instead, make your Yelp page prominent in your space and let quality experiences drive natural reviews.

**3. Mindbody / ClassPass (if applicable)**
Why it matters: Mindbody is a discovery platform — members already on the platform search for studios in their area. ClassPass brings you trial members who are actively exploring studios. Both have internal search algorithms that surface studios based on profile completeness, pricing, and review volume.
How to claim or set up: business.mindbody.io for Mindbody; partners.classpass.com for ClassPass.
What to optimize: Complete class descriptions with benefit-oriented language. High-quality photos (your best 10 from the photo shoot). Verified reviews. Current class schedule. If you use Mindbody as your scheduling software, your Google profile booking button should link directly to your Mindbody booking page.

**4. Facebook Business Page**
Why it matters: Facebook pages rank in Google search results for branded queries ("[Studio Name] reviews"). They also provide a trust signal — many potential members check a business's Facebook page to see activity, community engagement, and older reviews. Facebook's local awareness ads require an active Business Page to run.
How to claim or set up: facebook.com/pages/create. Fill out the About section completely (address, phone, website, hours, description). Use the same business name, address, and phone as your Google profile (NAP consistency).
What to optimize: Profile photo (logo), cover photo (studio interior or class action shot), full About section, Services tab, Reviews enabled. Post at minimum 2x per week — repurpose content from Instagram to keep it active.

**5. Bing Places for Business**
Why it matters: Bing powers search on all Microsoft devices, including Windows computers used by millions. It also powers some Cortana and Siri results. While Google dominates, Bing accounts for a meaningful percentage of US searches — for a local studio, that can be dozens of potential members per month you're leaving on the table.
How to claim or set up: bingplaces.com. Log in with a Microsoft account. Import directly from Google Business Profile (this takes 2 minutes and copies all your existing data). Verify via phone or email.
What to optimize: Same priorities as Google Business. The import feature makes this a quick win — complete it once and check it quarterly.

## SECTION 3 — EMAIL LIST BUILDING FOR FITNESS STUDIOS

### How to Collect Emails

**Method 1 — Free Trial Sign-Up Form**

Place a sign-up form on your website's homepage, your "New Here?" or "Pricing" page, and as a pop-up that triggers after 30 seconds or on exit intent. The form should collect: First Name, Email Address, and optionally, "What's your primary fitness goal?" (dropdown with 4-5 options). Keep it short — every additional field reduces completion rates.

What to offer in exchange: A free first class, a discount on a first class or intro package, or a PDF guide ("5 Things to Know Before Your First Yoga Class" or "The Beginner's Guide to Reformer Pilates"). The incentive doesn't need to be expensive — it just needs to feel like a fair trade for their email.

Where to place it: Homepage header (above the fold), a floating banner at the bottom of all pages, as a pop-up after 30 seconds on any page, and directly on your pricing page above the pricing table. Your pricing page visitors are already interested — don't let them leave without getting their email.

Form headline that converts: "Try [Studio Name] Free" or "Claim Your First Class" performs better than "Sign Up for Our Newsletter." Lead with what they get, not what you want from them.

**Method 2 — Challenge Registration**

Run a 7-day, 14-day, or 30-day challenge (e.g., "7-Day Flexibility Challenge," "14-Day Core Reset," "30-Day New Member Challenge"). Every person who wants to participate registers with their email address. You then deliver the challenge content via email, which means you're not just collecting addresses — you're immediately engaging new subscribers with high-value content.

How to structure it: Create a simple landing page on your website or use a free tool like Mailchimp's landing page builder. Title: "[Challenge Name] — Register Here." Fields: Name and Email. After registration, send an automated confirmation email that delivers Day 1 of the challenge. Continue with daily or every-other-day emails for the challenge duration.

Challenge promotion: Announce via Instagram, your existing email list, Google Business post, and Facebook. Encourage current members to invite friends — the challenge is a natural word-of-mouth tool because people love doing challenges together.

The residual effect: Everyone who completes (or attempts) your challenge has a warm relationship with your studio before they ever walk in. Convert them at the end of the challenge with a limited-time intro offer: "Challengers who book their first class this week get 30% off."

**Method 3 — In-Studio Sign-Up**

Set up a physical sign-up method at your front desk — either a clipboard with a sign-up sheet or a tablet with a simple form open. Train your front desk staff (or yourself) to offer it naturally to every first-time visitor.

Script for front desk: "Before you head in, can I grab your email? We send out our weekly schedule and member-only specials — and as a new visitor, you'll get [a discount code / a free class credit / access to our Welcome Guide] when you sign up."

What to say to encourage sign-ups: Make the benefit concrete and immediate. "I'll send you a discount code right now" works better than "we'll keep you updated." If you can give them something they receive immediately (showing them you just sent a welcome email to their address), do it — it confirms the system works and they got their incentive.

What to offer: A one-time incentive like $10 off their next visit, a free add-on (rental mat, foam roller use, smoothie), or a free class. This doesn't need to be expensive — the goal is to get the email address while their experience is fresh.

Tablet setup: Use a simple Google Form or Typeform embedded in Safari on an iPad. Keep it locked to that page using Guided Access (in iPhone/iPad accessibility settings) so visitors can't navigate away from the form.

Manual entry for clipboard sign-ups: Assign one person to enter clipboard emails into your email platform at the end of each day. Old, stale emails lose engagement — enter them the same day or the next morning.

### What to Send and How Often

**Frequency recommendation: biweekly (every two weeks)**
Twice a month is frequent enough to stay top of mind and not so frequent that subscribers feel overwhelmed. If you have major announcements (a new program launching, a limited-time offer expiring), send an additional email — don't save it for the scheduled newsletter.

**What goes in each email (structure that works):**

1. Schedule update: Any schedule changes, new class times, or upcoming special classes (1-2 sentences, with a link to your full schedule).
2. One practical tip: A short, genuinely useful fitness or wellness tip (3-5 sentences). Not a sales pitch — something they could use today. This is why people stay subscribed.
3. One promotion: Current special offer, intro deal, referral incentive, or upcoming workshop. Keep this to 3-4 sentences with a clear call to action and link.
4. One member story: 3-5 sentences about a real member's experience or result (with their permission). Use their first name. This humanizes your studio and builds social proof.

**Subject line formulas that work for fitness studios:**

Formula 1 — Curiosity gap: "[Something unexpected] about [common fitness assumption]"
Formula 2 — Numbered list: "5 reasons your [yoga / pilates / workout] isn't working yet"
Formula 3 — Direct benefit: "How to [achieve specific outcome] in [timeframe]"
Formula 4 — Personalization: "[First name], this is for you if [specific situation applies]"
Formula 5 — Social proof: "What [member first name] said after [X] classes"

**3 examples of strong subject lines:**

1. "The one thing most beginners get wrong in their first yoga class"
2. "Your free class expires this Sunday — here's how to book it"
3. "Sarah lost 18 pounds in 3 months. Here's what she did differently."

**Technical setup:**
Use Mailchimp (free up to 500 contacts), Flodesk (flat monthly fee, beautiful templates), or ConvertKit (good for automations). Set up a double opt-in to confirm email addresses — this keeps your list clean and improves deliverability.

## SECTION 4 — LOCAL PARTNERSHIPS FOR FITNESS STUDIOS

### Best Partner Types and Specific Ideas

Local partnerships are one of the highest-ROI marketing activities available to a small fitness studio because they put your name in front of people who are already invested in their health. The trust transfer is built in — when a physical therapist recommends your studio, their patient arrives with far more credibility assigned to you than a cold Instagram ad could ever create.

---

**Physical Therapist / Chiropractor**

Why they're a perfect referral partner: Their patients are recovering from injury, managing chronic pain, or working to maintain functional mobility — exactly the population that benefits most from guided yoga, pilates, or corrective movement programming. Their patients have already overcome the "I should exercise" barrier and are actively investing in their bodies.

What to offer them: Propose a professional referral arrangement, not a financial kickback (which can be legally problematic in healthcare contexts). Instead: offer to refer your members to their practice when they need clinical care. Offer to host a free "Yoga for Back Pain" or "Pilates for Injury Recovery" workshop at your studio that they can promote to their patients. Offer a complimentary intro class for the PT or chiropractor themselves so they can speak from personal experience when recommending you.

Specific cross-referral arrangement: Create a "Referred by [PT Practice Name]" landing page or discount code. When the physical therapist gives their patient a business card or brochure from your studio, that card includes a specific code or URL. This makes the referral trackable and shows the PT that it's actually working. In return, keep a stack of the PT's business cards at your front desk and train your staff to mention them when members discuss injury, chronic pain, or needing clinical assessment.

Joint content idea: Co-produce a short video or blog post series: "[PT Name] and [Studio Name] Answer: What's the difference between physical therapy and yoga for back pain?" This content educates both audiences, lives on both websites, and establishes both parties as trusted authorities. Post it on YouTube, share via email, and cross-post on Instagram.

---

**Registered Dietitian / Nutritionist**

Partnership ideas:

Joint workshop: "Move Well, Eat Well" — a 90-minute workshop at your studio combining a movement class taught by you and a nutrition Q&A taught by the dietitian. Charge a nominal fee ($25-35) or offer it free to members and paid to non-members (which brings new people through your door). The dietitian promotes it to their client list; you promote it to yours.

Newsletter feature: Each month, feature a short nutrition tip contributed by your dietitian partner in your email newsletter. In exchange, they include a fitness tip from you in their newsletter or social content. Both audiences get value; both brands get reach.

Referral arrangement: Offer members who are interested in nutrition a direct referral to your partner dietitian. Ask the dietitian to do the same — when a client mentions they want to add movement to their lifestyle, you're the first name they mention.

What to offer the dietitian: A free 3-month membership to your studio (so they can practice and speak from personal experience), and co-branded content that features their expertise on your platform (which grows their audience).

---

**Healthy Meal Prep Service / Juice Bar**

Cross-promotion: Offer studio members a discount at the meal prep service or juice bar (10-15%), and ask the meal prep service or juice bar to offer their customers a discount at your studio (free first class, or 20% off a week pass).

How to execute: Create a co-branded flyer or card using Canva. Your studio distributes the meal prep discount; the meal prep service distributes the studio discount. Print 200 cards each. This is an evergreen arrangement — replenish cards quarterly.

Co-created content: "Fuel Your Practice" — a joint social media series featuring post-workout meals, pre-class snacks, and hydration tips. Each brand creates content that features the other's product or service. For example: a smoothie brand creates a "post-yoga recovery smoothie" reel that tags your studio and drives viewers to book. You create a post-class wellness routine that includes one of their menu items.

Pop-up collaboration: Invite the juice bar to set up a table at your next community event (free outdoor class, open house, studio anniversary). They sell product; you get added value for attendees. Both brands cross-post the event.

---

**Local Running Club / Sports Team**

How to connect: Most cities have free running clubs that meet at a local running store or park 2-3 times per week. Find them via Meetup.com, local Facebook groups, or by asking at a running store. Sports teams (adult recreational leagues, cycling clubs, swim teams) can be found through city parks and recreation departments or through platforms like ZogSports or local community boards.

What to offer: A recovery class or programming specifically designed for runners, cyclists, or their sport. "Yoga for Runners" is a well-established format that directly addresses tight hamstrings, IT bands, and hip flexors — problems every runner faces. Offer to teach a single complimentary recovery session for the club or team as an introduction. Ask nothing in return except permission to leave business cards and offer a member discount.

Community event opportunities: Sponsor a post-race or post-game recovery session. If a local 5K or 10K happens in your neighborhood, set up a booth or host a free stretch session at the finish line. This puts your brand in front of hundreds of already-motivated fitness consumers.

Strength for endurance athletes: If you offer strength training or resistance-focused classes, pitch a "Strength for Runners/Cyclists" program — a 6-week small group training specifically designed to reduce injury and improve performance for endurance athletes. This is a premium, differentiated offering that commands higher price points.

---

**Corporate HR Departments**

How to pitch corporate wellness: Identify employers within 1-2 miles of your studio or in your city with 50+ employees. Reach out to the HR Director or Head of People directly via LinkedIn or email. Your pitch should be brief and outcome-focused: reduced stress, improved productivity, and a valuable employee benefit that costs less than traditional gym stipends.

What to include in the pitch: One page or email maximum. Include: what you offer (class types, schedule, format), what employees get (group membership rate, private corporate classes on-site or at the studio, wellness challenges), and a cost estimate. Include one or two testimonials if you have them. Attach your most compelling class photo.

Pricing structure for group/corporate memberships: Offer a 15-25% discount off your standard unlimited membership rate for corporate accounts with a minimum commitment of 5 employees. For companies that want private sessions (you bring a class to their office once a month), price at your hourly group rate plus a travel fee. Require a 3-month minimum contract to make the arrangement worthwhile.

The HR department framing: Corporate HR teams respond to "reduced turnover," "mental health support," "stress management," and "team culture." Use this language in your pitch. "Our monthly yoga session has become one of the benefits employees mention most in our engagement surveys" is more persuasive than "your team will love yoga."

---

**Sports Apparel or Athletic Gear Shop**

Cross-referral display: Ask a local running store, athletic gear boutique, or outdoor sports shop if you can place a small display or card holder near their checkout counter. In exchange, feature their logo or products in your studio (a display near the entrance, or a mention in your newsletter). This is a natural, non-competitive partnership — their customers are exactly the people who would join your studio.

Joint event: Co-host a "Gear Up and Move" event — a gear demo or sale at your studio, combined with a free movement class. The gear shop brings products; you provide the venue and the class. Both brands promote it. It drives foot traffic for both parties and creates content for both social accounts.

Exclusive member discount: Negotiate a standing discount (10-15%) for your members at the gear shop. Feature it in your welcome email sequence, monthly newsletter, and in your studio via a small display. In return, offer their staff and customers a free trial class. This adds perceived value to your membership without costing you anything and gives the gear shop a reason to recommend you.

## SECTION 5 — COMMUNITY BUILDING TACTICS

### Free Community Workout Events

A free outdoor class or community workout is one of the most effective local marketing activities a fitness studio can run. It delivers real value, creates content, builds goodwill, and fills your email list — all at once.

**Logistics checklist:**

Planning (2-4 weeks before the event):
- Choose a venue: local park, waterfront area, community plaza, parking lot. Check if a permit is required — many parks require one for organized events over 25 people. Call your city parks department; it's usually $25-50 and straightforward.
- Set a date and time: Weekend mornings (8am-10am) work best for yoga and general fitness events. Schedule it at least 3 weeks out to allow adequate promotion time.
- Choose a format: 45-60 minutes is ideal. Longer and attendance drops off. A single format (yoga, bootcamp, HIIT) works better than mixing styles at the same event.
- Decide on capacity: 30-50 people is manageable for a first event. Use a free Eventbrite or Google Form registration to track sign-ups and collect emails.
- Arrange for music: A Bluetooth speaker is sufficient for outdoor events. Create a pre-made playlist before the event day.
- Bring supplies: Printed sign-up sheets for email collection, business cards, a banner or sign with your studio name visible, and a phone or camera for content capture.

Promotion (starting 2 weeks before):
- Create an event on Facebook Events and post in local Facebook groups ("Austin Events," "[Neighborhood] Community," "Austin Fit Life," etc.)
- Promote on Instagram: 2-3 feed posts, daily stories in the final week, and a countdown sticker on the day before
- Send one dedicated email to your list specifically about this event
- Post on Google Business using the Event post type
- Print and distribute flyers at partner businesses (physical therapist, juice bar, running store)
- Post in Nextdoor for your neighborhood and adjacent neighborhoods

How to capture attendee info for follow-up:
Set up a clipboard at the check-in table with fields for name and email. Offer a small incentive for signing in: "Registered attendees are entered to win a free month membership." Assign one person to manage sign-in — not the instructor. After the event, enter all emails into your email platform within 24 hours and trigger the welcome sequence.

How to document it for content:
Assign a dedicated content person to photograph and video the event. They should not participate in the class — their only job is capturing content. Key shots: wide view of the group, close-ups of participants in strong poses, instructor leading from the front, participants smiling and talking before and after. Shoot in burst mode for movement shots. Film 15-30 second video clips for Reels and Stories. This single event should produce 2-3 weeks of social content.

Post-event follow-up:
Within 48 hours, send a follow-up email to all sign-ups: "Thank you for joining us at [event name]. Here's a special offer for attendees: [free first class / intro week discount / discount code]. This is our way of saying thank you for spending your morning with us." Include a direct booking link. This email converts event attendees into paying members while their experience is fresh.

### Studio Ambassador Program

An ambassador program turns your most enthusiastic current members into a structured word-of-mouth engine. The best referrals come from people who genuinely love your studio — an ambassador program gives those people a framework, recognition, and a reason to spread the word more actively.

**How to identify your best advocates:**
Look for members who: tag your studio in Instagram posts without being asked, leave reviews unprompted, bring friends to class on their own initiative, interact with your content consistently, say enthusiastic things to your staff, or have been members for 6+ months. Start with 3-5 people. A small, engaged group outperforms a large, lukewarm one every time.

**What to offer ambassadors:**
- A free month of membership for every [3 / 5] paying members they refer
- Branded merchandise (high-quality water bottle, tote bag, or tank top with your studio logo)
- Early access to new classes, workshops, or programs before they open to the general public
- A monthly "Ambassador" gathering or coffee chat with the owner or head instructor
- A social media feature (member spotlight post on your Instagram)

Keep the incentive meaningful but not purely transactional. The best ambassadors are people who love your studio and want to share it — you're rewarding that enthusiasm, not buying fake endorsements.

**What you ask in return:**
- 1-2 genuine Instagram posts per month (their own content, not scripted — just ask them to tag you when they post about fitness)
- A Google review (once, not repeatedly — reviews from the same person don't have additional weight)
- To mention your studio to friends who ask about fitness in the area
- To be available occasionally for a photo or video for your content

Do not ask ambassadors to post scripted marketing language. Authentic posts from real members consistently outperform brand-scripted posts from those same people.

**How to onboard them:**
Have a conversation (in person, by phone, or via email) before making the formal offer. Something like: "I've noticed how consistently enthusiastic you are about this community, and I want to recognize that. Would you be interested in being a [Studio Name] Ambassador? Here's what it means..." Then explain the arrangement clearly. Give them a welcome package: branded merchandise, a written summary of what you're asking and what they receive, and a unique referral code or link to track their referrals.

**How to track results:**
Give each ambassador a unique discount code or referral link (most scheduling platforms support this). When a new member uses that code, the referral is attributed to that ambassador. Review referral counts monthly. At the end of each quarter, reward ambassadors who hit a referral threshold with a bonus incentive (additional free month, exclusive workshop access, gift card to a partner business).

### First-Class Experience Checklist (10 Points)

The first class experience is the most important marketing moment in your business. A new member who has a remarkable first experience will join, stay, and refer others. A new member who feels invisible, confused, or unwelcome may not come back — and you will never know why. Make these ten touchpoints intentional and consistent.

**1. Personalized greeting by name**
When a first-time member checks in (online booking systems show who's coming), your front desk should greet them by first name before they introduce themselves. "Are you [Name]? We've been looking forward to meeting you." This is a small act that signals they were expected and that they matter. Train every staff member on this habit.

**2. Studio orientation (3 minutes)**
Before the class begins, have a staff member or the instructor walk the new member through the studio: where to leave shoes and belongings, where the bathrooms are, where to find equipment, which area of the studio is best for beginners. Do not assume they know. Confusion and uncertainty in the first five minutes are the enemy of a good first-class experience.

**3. Instructor introduction before class**
The instructor should introduce themselves to first-time members before the class starts — by name, with a handshake or warm greeting. "I'm [Name] — I'll be leading today. Let me know if anything I cue isn't clear, and don't hesitate to rest anytime you need to." This removes the anxiety of being in a new class with a stranger leading it.

**4. Verbal acknowledgment at the start of class**
The instructor briefly acknowledges new faces at the start of class — without singling them out in an embarrassing way. "If this is your first time with us, welcome — this class is for every level, and I'll offer modifications for every movement." This normalizes being new and signals to the whole class that they're in a welcoming environment.

**5. Form check during class**
The instructor makes at least one personal contact with the new member during class — a gentle verbal cue, an alignment correction, or a word of encouragement. "You're doing really well — try shifting your weight back slightly." This feels like individual attention, not surveillance. It makes new members feel coached and seen.

**6. Modification demonstration**
Throughout class, the instructor demonstrates modifications for all key movements and explicitly invites students to use them. "This is the full expression of the pose — and here's the modification if your body isn't there yet. Both are completely appropriate." This prevents new members from feeling like they're failing when they can't do the advanced version.

**7. Post-class check-in**
Within 5 minutes of class ending, either the instructor or a staff member approaches the new member: "How did that feel? Was the pace okay? Do you have any questions?" This is the highest-value 60-second conversation in your business. It gives you immediate feedback and makes the new member feel seen. If they mention something that was hard, respond with empathy and a specific next step.

**8. Next class recommendation**
Do not let a new member leave without a specific next class recommendation. Not "come back anytime" — something specific: "Based on what you told me about your goals, I think you'd love [specific class name] on [specific day] at [specific time]. Can I help you book it before you leave?" Remove friction. Help them take the next step while the experience is fresh and motivation is at its peak.

**9. Community touchpoints**
If your studio has community boards, a member goal wall, challenge tracking, or events boards, briefly point them out. "We have a board back there where members post their goals and celebrate milestones — feel free to add yours." Small touches like this signal that your studio is a community, not a transaction, and give new members a reason to feel invested before they've even signed up.

**10. Digital follow-up within 24 hours**
Send a personal-feeling follow-up message within 24 hours of their first class. This can be an automated email (but write it to sound human, not like a form letter). "Hi [Name] — it was great having you in class today. How are you feeling? If you have any questions about your next steps, just reply to this email. We'd love to see you again." Include a direct booking link. This closes the loop on the first-class experience and bridges the gap to a second visit — which is typically the moment visitors convert into members.

## SECTION 6 — OFFLINE MARKETING THAT STILL WORKS

### Referral Cards for Members

Referral cards are one of the oldest and most reliable tools in local business marketing. They work because they remove friction from the referral process — instead of a member trying to describe your studio to a friend, they hand over a physical card that does it for them.

**Card design — what information to include:**
- Your studio name and logo on the front, prominent
- The offer for the new person: "Your First Class Free" or "Two Weeks for $[X]" — make it specific and compelling
- The offer for the referring member: "Give your friend a free class. You both win." or "When your friend joins, you get a free month."
- A unique referral code printed on the card so you can track which member the referral came from
- Your website URL and phone number
- Keep the card to standard business card size (2" x 3.5") — they fit in wallets and pockets and are more likely to actually be used

**How to distribute:**
- Give every current member 3-5 cards (not 20 — too many feels like spam and they'll never use them all)
- Include referral cards in the welcome package for every new member
- Leave a small stack at the front desk in a card holder
- Include a digital version (PDF or image) in your welcome email sequence so members can text it to friends

**Tracking method:**
Each referring member gets a unique code — something simple like their first name initial plus last name (e.g., JSARAH), or a sequential number. When a new member uses that code to book, the referral is logged. Most scheduling software (Mindbody, Acuity, Pike13) has a referral source field — use it consistently. At the end of each month, pull a referral report and deliver rewards to members who triggered conversions.

Your physical space communicates your brand and values before any staff member speaks. These four elements belong in your lobby or near your front desk.

Flyers are not glamorous, but they work — because they intercept people who are already in the right mindset about their health and fitness.

**Where to place them:**
- Physical therapy and chiropractic offices (waiting rooms — ask the front desk)
- Running stores and outdoor gear shops
- Health food stores and co-ops
- Juice bars and smoothie cafes
- Sports complexes and recreational centers
- Gyms that do not offer the same modality (a CrossFit gym won't compete with a yoga studio)
- Community bulletin boards at coffee shops, libraries, and laundromats

**What to include on the flyer:**

- Your strongest offer: "First Class Free" with the booking URL below it
- 1-3 sentences describing who you are and who it's for (not a full paragraph)
- Your address and phone number
- A QR code that links directly to your booking page or intro offer landing page
- Keep it visually clean — too much text means it won't be read

**How to track which locations drive traffic:**
Create a unique landing page or discount code for each location. For example:
- Flyers at the physical therapy office use code: PT25 (25% off first class)
- Flyers at the running store use code: RUN25
- Flyers at the health food store use code: HFS25

When new members use these codes to book, you know exactly which flyer location drove the visit. After 90 days, review which locations drove the most sign-ups and prioritize replenishing those. Pull flyers from locations that drove zero conversions and test a different placement.

### Window Signage

Your windows are valuable advertising space visible to every person who walks, drives, or bikes past your location.

**"Free Trial" or "New Members Welcome" with QR code**
A clean, well-designed window cling or vinyl decal. Headline: "New Members Welcome — First Class Free." Below it: a large QR code linking to your intro offer page. This is always-on advertising that works 24 hours a day. It also signals to prospective members walking by that there is a no-risk way to try your studio — removing the "I'll check it out eventually" delay.

**Seasonal campaign window graphics**
For major seasonal campaigns (New Year new member drive, back-to-school fitness push, summer challenge), update your window graphics with seasonal creative. A vinyl decal printer can produce window graphics for $40-150 depending on size. Canva has window graphic and poster templates you can customize. Updating seasonally signals that your studio is active and current — a window that hasn't changed in two years suggests neglect.

**How to make them without a designer (step by step):**
1. Go to canva.com and open a free account
2. Search for "window sign" or "poster" templates — a standard 24" x 36" poster format works for most window displays
3. Choose a template that uses your brand colors and a clean, bold font
4. Replace the template text with your offer and QR code
5. Generate a QR code at qr-code-generator.com or qrcode-monkey.com linking to your booking page
6. Download the Canva design as a PDF at print quality
7. Upload the PDF to a local print shop (Staples, FedEx Office, or a local printer) and order a 24x36 poster print on glossy paper or foam board, or order a window cling

For window clings that adhere directly to glass, use a local sign shop — search "[city] sign printing" or "[city] vinyl decals." A professional cling typically runs $50-150 and lasts 1-2 years outdoors.

## SECTION 7 — 90-DAY LOCAL MARKETING PLAN

### Month 1 — Foundation

**Week 1:**
1. Complete the Google Business Profile audit. Work through every item in the checklist above. Budget 90 uninterrupted minutes. Do not move on until every section is filled in.
2. Access your Google review link. Go to your Google Business dashboard, click "Ask for reviews," copy the URL, shorten it at bit.ly, and test it from a different device. Save this link in a document you can access quickly from your phone.
3. Schedule a photo shoot for the coming week. Line up a friend, a staff member, or a member who is skilled with a camera. Book 2 hours at the studio during a live class (for action shots) and 30 minutes before or after for empty-studio and equipment shots.
4. Set up your email capture method. Install your pop-up form on your website (Mailchimp and ConvertKit both have native website pop-up tools). Test it on mobile to verify it works. Set up the welcome email sequence with Emails 1, 2, and 3 from this playbook — all three should be loaded and active before you drive any new traffic.
5. Place a sign-up clipboard or tablet at your front desk. Brief your staff (or yourself) on the script for encouraging sign-ups from every first-time visitor.
6. Create a Canva account and explore referral card templates. Design one referral card template even if you don't print it this week.
7. Research the 5 directories. Check whether your studio is already listed on Google, Yelp, Mindbody/ClassPass, Facebook, and Bing. Make a list of which ones need to be created or claimed.

**Week 2:**
1. Publish your first Google Business post. Use the Class Spotlight format: 1 photo, 2-3 sentences about a specific class, link to book. This takes 10 minutes. The goal this week is just to post, not to post perfectly.
2. Activate your email welcome sequence. Send yourself a test email to verify all three emails arrive correctly, the links work, and the formatting looks right on a mobile device. Fix any issues before real subscribers trigger the sequence.
3. Schedule your first week of social content from your content calendar. Batch-schedule 3-5 posts using Later, Buffer, or your platform's scheduling tool. Content creation and distribution should happen in dedicated blocks, not reactively throughout the week.
4. Upload the photos from your Week 1 photo shoot to your Google Business Profile. Add at minimum 5 photos — at least one exterior, two interior, and two class-in-action shots.
5. Create and submit your Yelp business profile if not yet done. Complete all fields and upload 10 photos. Ensure name, address, and phone number match your Google profile exactly.
6. Claim or update your Facebook Business Page. Verify that the name, address, phone, and hours match your Google profile exactly. Enable the Reviews tab if it's not already on.
7. Print 50 referral cards (or order them from Canva Print or Vistaprint — 3-5 business day turnaround for $15-25). Place them in a card holder at the front desk.

**Week 3:**
1. Draft a list of 5-10 local partner businesses. Include: 1-2 physical therapy or chiropractic offices, 1-2 health food or meal prep businesses, 1 running store or sports shop, 1 registered dietitian or nutritionist, and 1 corporate HR contact or local employer.
2. Send your first partnership outreach email. Choose the business that feels most aligned with your clientele and send the template from Section 4. Personalize the first two sentences with something specific about their business.
3. Identify 3-5 members who could be ambassadors. Note who tags you unprompted, who brings friends, who has left reviews, who has been a consistent member for 6+ months. Do not approach them yet — just identify them this week.
4. Set up your Bing Places for Business profile. Import from Google Business Profile at bingplaces.com (takes 5 minutes). Verify via phone or email. This is a one-time task.
5. If you use ClassPass or Mindbody, log into your provider dashboard and ensure your profile is fully complete: photos uploaded, class descriptions written with benefit-oriented language, pricing current and accurate.
6. Write and post 2 pre-populated Q&A entries on your Google Business Profile. Log out of your business account, ask the question from your personal Google account, then log back in as the business owner to answer.
7. Begin requesting Google reviews from your 5-10 most loyal current members. Send individual, personal messages — not a bulk email. A text message with your shortened review link is highly effective. Keep it casual: "Hey [Name] — would you have 2 minutes to leave us a Google review? Here's the direct link: [URL]. It really helps new people find us."

**Week 4:**
1. Record your current review count on Google and Yelp. Write it down or put it in a spreadsheet. This is your baseline. You'll measure growth from here at the end of Month 3.
2. Approach your first ambassador candidate. Have a genuine in-person conversation. Make the offer, explain what you're asking and what they receive, and give them 48 hours to think about it without pressure.
3. Review and update your Yelp profile. Respond to any existing reviews — thank positive ones, address negative ones professionally and concisely. Check that your description, photos, and hours are accurate and current.
4. Send partnership follow-up emails to any businesses you contacted in Week 3 who haven't responded. One follow-up is appropriate and expected; two is the maximum. If no response after two contacts, move on.
5. Add 3 more pre-populated Q&A entries to your Google Business Profile. You should now have 5 of the recommended 8.
6. Create your "Flyer Drop" target list: write down 5 businesses in your neighborhood where you want to place flyers. Research whether they have community bulletin boards or will accept a small display near the door.
7. Design your studio flyer in Canva and print 50-100 copies. Black and white is acceptable for community bulletin boards; full color is better for partner business displays.

### Month 2 — Community

**Week 5:**
1. Choose a date, venue, and format for your community event (free outdoor class or workshop). Book the venue or confirm park permit requirements.
2. Create the event on Facebook Events and Eventbrite. Write a compelling event description that includes what people will do, who it's for, what to bring, and how to register.
3. Publish your first community event promotional post on Instagram as a feed post (not just a story). Include all key logistics — date, time, location, what to bring — in the caption.
4. Send a dedicated email to your current subscriber list announcing the event. Include all logistics and a link to register. A dedicated email (not combined with the newsletter) signals that this is significant.
5. Post the event on Google Business using the Event post type.
6. Begin posting event promotions in relevant local Facebook groups — post once per group, do not repeatedly repost in the same group.
7. Drop event flyers at the partner businesses and complementary locations you identified in Month 1.

**Week 6:**
1. Send a follow-up email to any partnership prospects who haven't yet responded to your Week 3 outreach. If you have one confirmed partner, draft your joint promotion together — even a simple co-branded referral card is a strong start.
2. Finalize the confirmed partnership. If it's a referral card arrangement, design the cards and agree on a distribution date. If it's a joint event, confirm the date and begin co-promoting to both audiences.
3. Send your first full email newsletter to your existing subscriber list. This is different from the welcome sequence — it goes to everyone on your list. Use the structure from Section 3: schedule update, one tip, one promotion, one member story.
4. Continue promoting the community event on Instagram stories daily in the final 5 days leading up to it. Use the countdown sticker on your story the day before.
5. Prepare your check-in setup for the community event: table, sign, clipboards with sign-up sheets, business cards, a device for photos.
6. Brief your content person for the event. Give them a specific shot list: crowd wide shot, instructor action shot, close-ups of participants, and 3-5 post-event social moments.
7. Post 2 more Q&A entries on Google Business. You should now have 7 of the recommended 8.

**Week 7:**
1. Identify and repost your first piece of user-generated content. Search for any posts that tag your studio or use your location tag. Ask the member for permission via a DM, then repost with credit and a genuine caption. This is both content and social proof.
2. Officially launch your ambassador program with 2-3 members. Have a personal conversation with each person. Give them their welcome package: merchandise, referral code, and a written summary of the arrangement. Set expectations clearly and warmly.
3. Set your Google review count target for the end of Month 3. A reasonable target: current count plus 15 reviews over the next 6-8 weeks. Write this number down.
4. Post your eighth and final pre-populated Q&A entry on Google Business. All 8 questions are now answered and owned by you.
5. Upload new photos to your Google Business Profile. If event photos are ready, upload at least 5 from the community event. Photos of real people at real events perform exceptionally well.
6. Check your email welcome sequence performance metrics in your email platform. If your open rate is below 30%, test a new subject line for Email 1. If your click-through rate on the booking link is below 5%, rewrite the call-to-action sentence to be more direct.
7. Send a "community event this week" reminder email to your full list 48 hours before the event.

**Week 8:**
1. Hold your community event.
2. Collect emails from every attendee at the check-in table. Have one staff member or volunteer dedicated to this role throughout the event.
3. Ensure your content person captures photos and video throughout the event — wide group shots, instructor action, and informal post-class moments.
4. After the event, enter all new email addresses into your email platform within 24 hours. Tag them as "Event Attendee" for segmentation so you can target them specifically later.
5. Within 24-48 hours, send a post-event follow-up email to all registrants and attendees. Thank them for coming, share one highlight photo, and include a time-limited intro offer exclusive to event attendees. "This offer is only for people who joined us at [event name] — it expires [specific date]."
6. Post event content on Instagram: a Reel or carousel featuring the best event photos. Tag any participants who agree to being tagged. Write a caption that makes non-attendees feel like they missed something they'll want to experience next time.
7. Send a brief thank-you message or email to any partner businesses who helped promote the event. Maintain these relationships — they're worth far more than a single event.

### Month 3 — Growth

**Weeks 9-10:**
1. Launch your first paid local awareness campaign on Facebook and Instagram. Recommended budget: $5-10 per day for 14 days ($70-140 total). This is a test, not a major investment — the goal is to learn what resonates.
   - Campaign objective: Awareness (for reach) or Traffic (for clicks to your booking page)
   - Audience: Location set to a 5-mile radius around your studio address. Age and gender matching your target member profile. Exclude people who already follow your page — they know you.
   - Creative: Your single best class action photo, or a 15-30 second Reel showing the studio energy
   - Ad copy: Lead with the free trial or intro offer. Example: "Try [Studio Name] free. [City]'s boutique [yoga / pilates / training] studio for people who want [outcome]. Book your first class this week — no experience needed." CTA button: "Book Now" linking to your intro offer landing page.
2. Check ad performance every 2-3 days. If cost per link click is over $3, test a new image creative. If reach is lower than expected, broaden the age range by 5 years on each end. Do not make multiple changes at once — change one variable at a time so you know what made the difference.
3. Continue posting weekly on Google Business throughout both weeks. Do not let the ad campaign distract you from organic channels.
4. Your ambassadors should now be active for 3+ weeks. Check their referral code usage in your scheduling platform. If no referrals have come in yet, send each ambassador a personal check-in: "Hey [Name] — how are things going with the ambassador program? Is there anything I can do to make it easier?" Sometimes the friction is simple (they lost their referral code, they're unsure how to bring it up naturally).
5. Approach 2-3 additional businesses from your partner outreach list if you haven't yet confirmed 2 active partnerships. Diversifying your referral sources takes time — the pipeline needs to stay active.

**Week 11:**
1. Announce your referral program formally to your entire member list via email. Subject line example: "Know someone who'd love [Studio Name]? Here's what's in it for both of you." Body: explain the offer clearly and concisely, attach or display the referral card image, give them their unique code or a general code, and make it dead simple to share. Include the referral link they can forward directly.
2. Process ambassador rewards for Month 2. Deliver free class credits, merchandise, or free month memberships personally or via email with a note of genuine gratitude. Public acknowledgment (with permission) — a social post, a mention in the newsletter — means more to most ambassadors than the material reward.
3. Send your second full email newsletter to your entire list. Include: results from the community event (how many people attended, a highlight photo and moment), a tip relevant to the current season, your current promotion, and one member story. Your list has grown since the first newsletter — some of these subscribers are hearing from you for the first time since they signed up.
4. Pull your referral tracking report. How many new members came from referral cards? From ambassador codes? From flyers (tracked by discount code)? From the community event? From the paid ad campaign? This data tells you where your word-of-mouth and marketing efforts are actually converting.
5. Drop fresh flyers at your active partner business locations. Replenish any displays that are running low. Add 2-3 new locations you haven't tried yet.
6. Run a focused Google review push: text your most enthusiastic members personally with your Google review link and a specific ask. "Hey [Name] — I have a favor to ask. Would you mind leaving us a Google review? It genuinely helps new people find us. Here's the direct link: [URL]. It takes about 2 minutes and means a lot."
7. Upload at least 5 new photos to your Google Business Profile this week — event photos, any new team shots, or updated studio photos.

**Week 12:**
1. Conduct your 90-day metrics review. Pull and record these numbers:
   - Google Business: Total review count now vs. baseline recorded in Month 1, Week 4. Profile views over the last 30 days vs. the first 30 days of the campaign.
   - Email list: Total subscribers now vs. Day 1. Average open rate across all newsletters and sequences. Average click-through rate on booking links.
   - New member sign-ups: Total new memberships over the 90 days. How many came from referral? From Google? From the community event? From the paid ad? From flyers (by discount code)? From ClassPass or Mindbody?
   - Social: Follower count now vs. 90 days ago. Top 3 posts by reach over the period. Engagement rate trend (improving, flat, or declining).
   - Referral and ambassador: Total referrals tracked. Revenue attributable to the referral card program and ambassador program combined.
2. Identify your top 3 marketing channels by new member attribution. Double down on those in Quarter 2 — more time, more budget, more optimization.
3. Identify your bottom 2 channels by performance. Decide: test a new variation for another 90 days, or reallocate the time and budget to higher-performing channels. Not every channel will work in every market.
4. Draft your Quarter 2 local marketing plan. You now have 90 days of real data from your specific market. Build the next quarter's plan around what you've proven — not a wish list based on what works for other studios.
5. Schedule your next community event for Month 4 or Month 5. You now have the full logistics playbook and know what drove the best turnout and follow-up conversion from the first event.
6. Recognize and reward your ambassadors for the full quarter. A personal note, a bonus class credit, or a small celebration (an ambassador appreciation coffee before or after class) goes a long way toward keeping the best ones engaged for the long term.
7. Take stock of what you've built: a complete Google Business Profile, an active email list with a functioning welcome sequence, at least 2 local partnerships, an ambassador program, one community event completed, a referral system in operation, and a paid advertising baseline. Most fitness studios never build this foundation systematically. The ones that do grow steadily for years — not because they went viral, but because they showed up consistently in the right places for the right people.
